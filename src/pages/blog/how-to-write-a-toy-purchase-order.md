**Short answer:** a purchase order is not a price confirmation. It is the document that decides what happens when production differs from expectation — which is why it has to name the sample, the carton, the artwork version and the inspection standard, not just the item and the quantity.

## A description is not a specification

"500 pcs of the bubble gun, as quoted" is a description. It resolves nothing when the goods arrive in a different colour box, because there was never a statement of which box.

A specification names the things that can vary: colour reference, material, dimensions, packaging type, artwork file and version, label text, carton configuration. Every one of those is a decision someone made; the purchase order should record it.

## The twelve lines

1. **Item number** exactly as quoted.
2. **Quantity**, and whether partial shipment is allowed.
3. **Unit price and trade term** (FOB port, CIF port, EXW).
4. **Sample reference** — the date the approved sample was received.
5. **Colour and material**, by reference to the sample or a named standard.
6. **Packaging**: retail box, poly bag, sleeve; and the artwork file name and version.
7. **Carton configuration**: pieces per carton, carton dimensions if agreed, and the carton marking.
8. **Label and warning text**, quoted verbatim, or the approved artwork reference.
9. **Inspection standard**: who inspects, the AQL level, and the point at which inspection happens.
10. **Delivery date**, defined as the date the goods are ready for shipment rather than the date they arrive.
11. **Payment terms** with the trigger for each payment.
12. **Documents required**: invoice, packing list, bill of lading, and the conformity documents for the destination.

If any of those is missing, someone will fill the gap by assumption, and the assumption will not be yours.

## The clause buyers forget

**What happens if the inspection fails.** Most purchase orders say an inspection will happen; very few say what follows a failure. Two sentences fix it: the supplier reworks or replaces at their cost before shipment, and the balance is not due until a passed inspection.

Without that clause, a failed inspection becomes a negotiation in which you hold no cards — the goods are made, the money is committed, and the ship date is your problem too.

## Dates: ready, not arrived

A delivery date expressed as an arrival date puts the shipping risk on the supplier in a way they cannot control, so they will pad it. A date expressed as "ready for shipment by" is something production can actually commit to, and it lets you calculate the shipping leg yourself.

Ask for the ready date, then count the transit yourself. That also gives you the number you need for a seasonal deadline.

## Revision control

Any change after the purchase order — a colour tweak, a logo move, a packaging change — needs to come back as a written revision with a date, and both sides should be able to say which revision is in production.

The failure mode is predictable and common: a change agreed in a chat message, applied by the factory to some of the run, and discovered by nobody until the cartons are open. A one-line revision email prevents it.

## A short version for small orders

For a first order of two or three items, a full twelve-line document may be overkill. A single email with the item numbers, quantities, prices, the approved sample date, the carton configuration and one sentence on inspection covers most of the risk. What you should not skip is the sample reference and the inspection trigger — those are the two lines that decide who pays when something goes wrong.
