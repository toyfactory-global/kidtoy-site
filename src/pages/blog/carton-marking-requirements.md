**Short answer:** a toy export carton needs shipping marks for the forwarder, side marks for the warehouse, and the country of origin for customs. When they are missing, the shipment does not stop at the border — it stops at your warehouse, where somebody has to open cartons and identify them by hand.

## The two sets of marks

**Shipping marks** are for the transport chain: who the consignee is, the reference, the carton count, the dimensions and weight, and often a handling symbol. They let a forwarder and a customs broker identify the cargo without opening it.

**Side marks** are for your own operation: item number, description, quantity per carton, colour or variant, and the barcode your warehouse scans. These are the marks that make receiving fast.

Factories sometimes print only the first set, because that is what the forwarder asks for. The second set is what your 3PL will ask for, and it is the one to specify on the purchase order.

## The minimum, line by line

- Consignee name and destination port
- Purchase order or shipment reference
- Carton number out of total (1 of 28, 2 of 28)
- Item number and short description
- Quantity per carton, in pieces
- Gross weight and net weight, in kilograms
- Carton dimensions, in centimetres
- Country of origin
- Handling symbols where relevant (this way up, keep dry)
- A scannable barcode for warehouse receiving

If a carton arrives with only "MADE IN CHINA" and a carton number, every one of those has to be established by opening it.

## Country of origin is a legal mark, not a preference

Most markets require the country of origin to be marked, and in the US it is required on the article or its container in a conspicuous place. Getting it wrong is one of the cheaper mistakes to fix at the factory and one of the more expensive ones to fix at the destination, where it means re-labelling in a bonded facility.

## Barcodes: whose, and where

The retail barcode belongs on the retail packaging, not on the carton — unless your warehouse or marketplace requires a carton-level label. Two different codes doing two different jobs:

- **Retail barcode (EAN/UPC)** on the sellable unit, for the shop or the marketplace.
- **Carton label** on the outside, for your warehouse and sometimes for a marketplace's inbound shipment.

Specify both, and specify where each goes. "Barcode on the box" is ambiguous enough to be printed in the wrong place.

## When the marks are wrong

Three outcomes, in order of cost:

1. **Receiving slows down.** Someone opens cartons to identify them. Annoying, not fatal.
2. **A marketplace rejects the inbound shipment.** Prep requirements are strict and a mislabelled carton can be refused at the door.
3. **Customs queries the origin marking.** The container waits while the paperwork is resolved.

The first is the common one, which is why it gets tolerated — and why the second and third eventually surprise somebody.

## What to send the factory

A one-page carton marking sheet: an image of the carton face with each field positioned, the exact text for each field, and the barcode file. Text instructions alone produce inconsistent results, because "item number top left" means different things to different people.

Ask for a photo of a printed carton before the full run. A single photo catches reversed text, missing origin marks and barcodes printed on the wrong face — all of which are cheap to fix at that moment and impossible to fix later.
