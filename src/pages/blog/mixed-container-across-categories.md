**Short answer:** a mixed container is worth doing when one supplier holds several of your SKUs and the load still cubes out. It stops being worth doing when the SKUs come from several factories, because consolidation, extra handling and split liability usually cost more than the freight you saved.

## The arithmetic that decides it

A container is limited by two things: volume and weight. Whichever is reached first sets the cost per unit.

- **Cube out.** Light, bulky goods — plush, bubble toys, large boxed sets. You run out of space before you run out of weight allowance.
- **Weight out.** Dense goods — building blocks, die-cast, liquids. You hit the weight limit with space left over.

Mixing the two is the whole point. Plush on top of block cartons uses the space that weight alone would leave empty.

The practical target is high cubic utilisation without overloading the axle limits. If your loading plan reaches roughly the usable volume of the container and stays inside the weight allowance, the mix has worked.

## When one supplier is the answer

If a single factory makes bubble toys, water guns and block sets, a mixed container is straightforward: one set of documents, one inspection, one loading plan.

That is our own situation, and it is why mixed containers suit first orders. A buyer can test four categories at a moderate quantity each instead of committing a full container to one product they have never sold.

## When several factories make it worse

Combining SKUs from three factories in one booking introduces:

- **Consolidation cost.** Goods move to a consolidation warehouse, are received, checked, and re-loaded. That is handling, and handling is where damage happens.
- **Split responsibility.** If a carton is crushed, whose fault is it? With one factory, the answer is obvious.
- **Documentation.** Each origin factory's goods need their own invoice lines, packing lists and declarations. More lines, more chances of an error at the broker.
- **Inspection.** Each SKU needs its own sampling. Three factories means three inspection arrangements.
- **Delay propagation.** One factory late means the whole container waits, and the others' goods sit in a warehouse accruing cost.

LCL per factory and a clean FCL for the volume lines is often the cheaper structure, despite the higher per-CBM rate.

## The loading plan

Mixing carton sizes is a physics problem, not a spreadsheet one.

1. **Heaviest and most rigid at the floor**, spread across the container length rather than stacked in one corner. Weight distribution matters for the road leg as much as the sea leg.
2. **Similar carton sizes together.** A stack of identical cartons interlocks; a stack of mixed sizes shifts.
3. **Fill the gaps.** Void space lets the load move. Dunnage, air bags or the cartons themselves if they can be arranged to fit.
4. **Height uniformity.** A level top surface lets you use the full container height rather than stopping at the tallest stack's neighbour.
5. **Door end last.** Whatever you need first at destination should be nearest the doors — and nothing heavy should be able to fall when they open.

Ask the factory for a loading plan by carton count per SKU, not a promise that it will fit. A plan you can check is worth more than an assurance.

## Documents for a multi-SKU load

- **Commercial invoice** with one line per SKU, each with its own description and HS line.
- **Packing list** matching the loading plan, carton by carton.
- **Origin** stated per line if any SKU is not wholly made in one country.
- **Certificates** per product, not per container — a mixed load means several test reports travel together, and they must each cover their own SKU.
- **Carton marking** that distinguishes SKUs at a glance, because a mixed container is unpacked at speed.

## Quality control across a mixed load

Sampling is per SKU. A common mistake is to inspect "the container" and sample the largest line. The small line is the one that arrives wrong, because it got the least attention.

Set the AQL per SKU in the purchase order, and inspect each range separately.

## A workable first mixed order

For a buyer opening a new market, a sensible pattern is:

- Two or three volume lines that will definitely sell.
- One or two trial lines to test demand.
- Total volume that fills the container without forcing the trial lines to a quantity you cannot sell.

That structure is what our [first container checklist](/blog/first-container-checklist/) is written around, and it is the reason we keep a mixed-category catalogue rather than a single-range one.

The container is a unit of freight, not a unit of merchandising. Use it to move the goods you want, in the proportion you want, rather than letting it force a decision you would not otherwise make.
