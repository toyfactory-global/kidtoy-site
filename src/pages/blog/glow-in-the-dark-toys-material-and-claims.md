**Short answer:** glow-in-the-dark parts store light and release it; they do not generate it. The pigment inside decides how bright and for how long. Ask which pigment, ask for the glow duration, and put "charge under light first" on the box or in the listing — that one sentence prevents most complaints about this category.

## The two pigments

| | Zinc sulfide | Strontium aluminate |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Low | Several times higher |
| Typical glow colour | Green | Green, blue, aqua |
| Brightness | Modest | Much brighter |
| Duration after charging | Short | Long |
| Stability | Degrades with moisture over years | Stabler |

Almost every cheap glow toy is zinc sulfide. Almost every toy that genuinely glows for a long time is strontium aluminate. Both are legitimate; the problem is when a buyer pays for one and receives the other.

A third variant is a coated or painted glow layer over a normal part. It is the cheapest of all and it wears off at the edges where children handle the toy most.

## What "glow time" means

Charge duration and glow duration are two different numbers and factories quote them loosely.

- **Charging** — a few minutes under a bright lamp or direct sun is usually enough. Under a phone torch, expect longer.
- **Glowing** — decays quickly at first, then tails off. A part may be clearly visible for ten minutes and faintly visible for an hour.

Ask for both figures, and ask what light source was used. "Glows for hours" from a factory usually means "faintly visible in a dark room after an hour, if you look hard".

## Why the claim matters commercially

Customers read "glow in the dark" as "lights up". They then take the toy into a dark room straight out of the box and see nothing, because it has never been charged. That is a review of one star, and it is entirely avoidable.

Three things fix it:

1. **Wording.** "Glow parts — charge under light first" on the box, the listing and the instruction sheet.
2. **A demonstration image.** A photo of the part charged versus uncharged, side by side. This is the single most effective piece of content for a glow product.
3. **Photography discipline.** Glow cannot be shot under studio light. It needs a dark capture with the part pre-charged — which means the sample has to be charged before the shoot, not during it.

## Safety is unchanged

A glow part is still a toy part and is tested the same way. Two points specific to this category:

- **The pigment is an additive.** Migration and heavy-metal testing applies to the material as supplied, glow pigment included. Very cheap pigment is where contamination risk concentrates, which is one reason the material cost differs so much.
- **Glow parts are usually small.** Claws, spines and antennae are the shapes that break off. Small-parts and torque tests matter more here than on a solid-bodied toy.

If a factory cannot say which pigment it uses, treat the safety documentation for that part as unverified.

## Moulded in or applied?

Three ways a part can glow:

1. **Moulded in the pigmented resin** — the most durable. The colour is through the part, and wear does not remove it.
2. **Two-shot moulding** — a glow section bonded to a structural part. Durable, and more expensive tooling.
3. **Painted or dipped** — cheapest, and it wears at edges and contact points.

For a toy that will be handled hard, ask for option one or two. For a display model, option three is acceptable if the price reflects it.

## What to ask before ordering

1. Which pigment — zinc sulfide or strontium aluminate.
2. Charge time and glow duration, with the light source used stated.
3. Moulded-in, two-shot, or applied.
4. Whether the glow material appears on the test report as a separate material.
5. Instruction wording for charging, in each language you sell.
6. A pre-charged dark photograph for your listing.

## Where this fits in a range

Glow is a differentiator that costs relatively little on a small part and reads strongly in a photo — which is exactly what marketplace listings need. It suits dinosaurs, space, insects and anything with spines or panels.

The honest framing, in our experience, is to sell it as a feature and never as a light. A child who is told the toy glows in the dark after being charged under a lamp is delighted. A child who is told it lights up is disappointed in ninety seconds.
