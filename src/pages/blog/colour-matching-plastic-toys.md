**Short answer:** a Pantone number tells the factory roughly what you want. It cannot be matched exactly on plastic, because pigment behaves differently in ABS, PP and TPE, because gloss changes perceived colour, and because each production batch is a small chemistry experiment. What protects you is a physical master sample plus a written tolerance — not a colour code.

## Why the same code gives different parts

**Material.** The same pigment in ABS, polypropylene and a soft TPE produces three visibly different results. TPE especially reads darker and less saturated because light scatters inside it.

**Surface.** Gloss and matte finish change apparent colour. Two parts moulded from the same resin, one polished and one textured, will not look like a matched pair.

**Pigment type.** Solid pigment, metallic and pearlescent pigments behave completely differently. Metallic colours vary with the viewing angle, so no single swatch can represent them.

**Thickness.** A thin wall transmits more light than a thick one. A translucent colour looks different on a 1 mm panel than on a 3 mm block.

**Batch.** Masterbatch dosing is measured, not exact. Small dose variation produces visible difference across large volumes.

This is why "match this Pantone" is an instruction with an implied asterisk, and why the asterisk is where arguments happen.

## The lab dip and the master sample

The process that works:

1. **Send the reference.** A Pantone chip, or a physical sample of the existing product.
2. **Ask for a colour chip or a first-shot part** in the actual material, at the actual finish.
3. **Approve physically, in daylight.** Not on a screen, not under a warm office lamp. North-facing daylight or a D65 viewing booth.
4. **Keep the approved sample sealed**, signed and dated, at both ends.
5. **Reference the sealed sample in the purchase order** as the colour standard.

Step four is the one people skip and the one that settles every dispute later. A sealed master at the factory and a duplicate with you is worth more than any specification.

## Writing a tolerance

Colour difference is measured as a delta value — how far one colour sits from another in a colour space. A smaller number means a closer match.

The useful structure for a toy order:

| Item | What to state |
|---|---|
| Standard | The sealed master sample, dated, with a photograph |
| Tolerance | A maximum colour difference per part, agreed with the factory |
| Lighting | The condition for judging — daylight or a specified illuminant |
| Measurement | Instrument type, and whether measured or visual |
| Exceptions | Parts where the tolerance is wider because of material |

A tolerance figure that both sides understand is more useful than a promise of "perfect match". Perfect does not exist across materials; a defined allowance does.

## Where colour goes wrong on a second order

Three recurring mechanisms:

**Regrind.** Factories reuse sprues and rejects by grinding them back into the resin. Regrind darkens and dulls colour, and the proportion is rarely stated. If colour consistency matters, ask what the regrind percentage is, and whether it is controlled.

**Pigment supplier change.** A different masterbatch supplier with the same nominal code can shift the result. This is also a compliance event, because the material changed.

**Mould wear and polish.** A worn cavity has a different surface texture, which changes gloss and therefore apparent colour, even with identical resin.

## Multi-part colour harmony

A toy usually has several parts in one colour family — a body, a lid, a trim. The three are often moulded by different machines or even different subcontractors.

To keep them consistent:

- Specify **one resin and one masterbatch batch** for all parts of the same colour.
- Mould the parts **in the same production window** where possible.
- Approve them **as an assembly**, not part by part. Individual approvals can all be "within tolerance" and still look wrong together.

That last point is the practical one. Judge the assembled product.

## Photography and the colour you promised

Even a perfect batch photographs wrong: white balance, screen calibration and platform compression all shift colour.

Practical protection:

- Shoot with a **grey card** in frame and correct white balance.
- Keep **one hero image per colourway** shot under controlled conditions, reused consistently.
- On marketplaces, expect some colour variance complaints regardless — a line in the listing ("colour may vary slightly between batches") heads off the worst of them.

## What to put in the purchase order

1. The colour reference: sealed sample, dated, plus a Pantone reference as a secondary note.
2. The tolerance, in a measured term both sides accept.
3. The regrind allowance, stated as a maximum percentage.
4. The requirement to notify any pigment supplier change before production.
5. A pre-production sample in the final material and finish, approved in daylight.

Colour is the first thing a customer judges and the last thing a factory controls exactly. The sealed sample is how you turn a vague expectation into something both sides can measure.
