**Short answer:** the case is the price point. Card boxes are the cheapest and the least giftable; aluminium and iron
cases photograph as "real makeup" and support a higher shelf price; ABS plastic sits between them and survives handling
better than card. Choose the case before the contents.

## The four formats

| Format | Cost | Gift appeal | Durability | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Printed card box | Lowest | Low | Dents, corners crush | Entry price, multipacks |
| ABS or PP plastic case | Low–mid | Mid | Good, scratches visible | Kids' first kit, marketplace |
| Aluminium case | Mid–high | High | Very good, hinge is the weak point | Gift retail, planogram |
| Iron / tinplate vanity | High | Highest | Best, heavy | Premium gift, trolley formats |

## Why aluminium reads as premium

A thin aluminium case has a metallic sheen, a satisfying weight in the hand, and a latch that clicks. None of those is
expensive to produce, but together they change the object's category in the customer's mind: it stops being a toy with
makeup in it and becomes a makeup case for a child.

The trade-off is that aluminium dents. A case that arrives with a dent in a retail carton is unsaleable, and aluminium
dents more easily than ABS under a point load. Inner packing matters more with aluminium than with any other format.

## Why card is still right for some ranges

A printed card box at a low price point is not a bad product; it is a different product. It works for a multipack, for a
party favour, for a marketplace listing where the box is photographed and then discarded, and for any price point under
about $6 retail where a case would eat the margin.

The mistake is putting a card box at an aluminium price, or an aluminium case at a card price and losing money on freight.

## Hinges, mirrors and latches

The three parts that fail, in order:

1. **The hinge.** On an aluminium case it is usually a piano hinge or two pins; on an ABS case it is a living hinge. A
   living hinge has a finite number of cycles and will crack — it is a design choice, not a defect.
2. **The mirror.** Glued mirrors come unglued in a warm container. A mirror held by a frame costs more and survives.
3. **The latch.** A friction latch that loosens over time is the difference between a case that closes and one that spills
   in a bag.

Ask how each is fixed. A supplier who can describe the hinge construction is a supplier who has thought about returns.

## Matching the case to the channel

- **Gift shop:** aluminium or iron, one hero format, one mid-price case. The case does the selling.
- **Pharmacy planogram:** consistent case footprint so it stacks; avoid trolley formats that do not stack.
- **Marketplace:** ABS or a rigid box that survives courier handling; a dented case is a return.
- **Party or multipack:** card, at a low unit cost, because the box is not the product.

## The freight angle

Cases are hollow, so they ship inefficiently: a case occupies volume without much weight. That makes the carton
dimensions and the nesting of the inner packing a real cost lever. A case that nests saves container space; a case with a
fixed handle does not.

This is worth raising at the sample stage, because inner-packing design is cheap to change before production and expensive
to change afterwards.

## What to check on a sample

Open and close the case fifty times. Look for hinge play, latch looseness and mirror movement. Drop it closed from waist
height. Then put a weight on it and leave it — the shape should recover or not deform.
