**Short answer:** for a children's product sold in the US, you need a Children's Product Certificate based on testing at a CPSC-accepted third-party laboratory, plus a permanent tracking label on the product and its packaging. Both are the importer's or domestic manufacturer's responsibility, and both get checked.

## The certificate is a summary, not evidence

The CPC is a one-page document that cites the rules the product was tested against. It is issued by the importer or domestic manufacturer — not by the lab — and it relies on the underlying test reports.

It has to state:

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| Product identification | Enough to match the shipment, including item number |
| Each rule cited | The specific regulation or ban the product was tested to |
| Importer or domestic manufacturer | Name, address, telephone |
| Contact for records | The person who holds the test reports |
| Manufacturing date and location | Where and when produced |
| Testing date and location | Where and when tested |

If any of those is missing or generic, the certificate is deficient even if the product is perfectly safe.

## Testing has to be at an accepted lab

The rules require third-party testing at a laboratory accepted by the CPSC for the specific tests. A factory's own in-house report, or a report from a lab that is not accepted for that test method, does not support a CPC.

For children's products, the recurring test set covers:

- **Lead in substrate** and **lead in paint or surface coating**.
- **Phthalates** in children's toys and child care articles, at the restricted level, for the specified phthalate list.
- **Small parts** for products intended for children under three.
- **Toy safety standard** requirements, where the product is a toy.
- **Flammability** where the material is in scope.
- **Tracking label** requirement, which is a labelling obligation rather than a test.

Which of these apply depends on the product and the age grade. The certificate has to cite the ones that do, and only the ones that do.

## The tracking label

This is the part most often done badly, because it is a printing decision rather than a testing one.

The label must be **permanent** and affixed to the product **and** its packaging, and must show:

1. Manufacturer or private labeler name.
2. Production location, including city and country at minimum.
3. Production date, including month and year.
4. Cohort information — a batch, run or lot identifier.
5. Any additional information that makes the specific unit traceable.

The purpose is recall traceability: in a recall, you must be able to identify which units came from which run.

Where it goes wrong:

- **On the box only.** The requirement covers the product as well. A toy inside a sealed colour box still needs the information on the toy or in a way that survives unpacking.
- **Using the item number as the only identifier.** An item number does not identify a production run.
- **Stickers on smooth plastic.** A sticker that peels is not permanent.
- **Missing city, only "China".** Country alone is not sufficient for the location element.

## The three gaps that stop shipments

**1. The certificate covers a different colourway or size.**
Testing families are legitimate, but the reports and the certificate must cover what is in the box. A pink variant tested and a blue variant shipped, with no documentation for the blue, is a gap.

**2. The age grade on the certificate differs from the packaging.**
If the product is certified as a toy for 3+ but labelled 0+, the small-parts analysis changes entirely and the certificate no longer describes the product as sold.

**3. The documentation is not available at the point of import.**
US Customs can request it, and marketplaces increasingly ask for it at listing creation. A PDF held by a trading company who is not the importer is not available when it is needed.

## Practical sequence for a first order

1. Agree the age grade in writing before sampling. It drives every test.
2. Get the lab's test plan and confirm it cites the current standard editions.
3. Receive the reports and check the sample photographs match your product.
4. Draft the CPC with your own details as importer.
5. Approve the artwork with the tracking label laid out, including the cohort field — which means the printer needs to be able to vary it per run.
6. Keep reports, CPC, and a retained sample together, retrievable for the retention period.

## What to ask a factory

1. Which accepted laboratory produced the reports, and for which tests.
2. The reports, complete, with sample photos.
3. The proposed tracking label layout, and how the cohort is printed per run.
4. Confirmation of manufacturing location and date format.
5. Advance notice of any material change that would require re-testing.

The certificate costs an afternoon and a lab invoice. The alternative, when a container is held, costs a season.
