**Short answer:** the machine is the durable good and the solution is the consumable — but the solution is what the
customer judges the machine by. A weak solution produces thin bubbles that pop immediately, and the machine gets the
blame, the review and the return.

## What bubble solution is

A bubble solution is water, a surfactant, a humectant and a small amount of polymer to give the film elasticity. The
surfactant lowers surface tension so a film can form; the humectant slows evaporation so the bubble survives longer; the
polymer helps the film stretch instead of tearing.

Cheap solution cuts the polymer and the humectant. It still bubbles, for about two seconds. That is enough to pass a quick
check at the factory and not enough to keep a child interested for an afternoon.

## Concentrate versus ready-mix

Two ways to buy, and the choice depends on your volume and your freight.

| | Ready-mix | Concentrate |
|---|---|---|
| What you ship | Water | Concentrate, diluted locally |
| Freight per litre of usable solution | High | Low |
| Mixing step | None | Yours, or the retailer's |
| Quality control | Fixed by the factory | Depends on the dilution |
| Best for | Small orders, retail packs | Distributors, large volumes |

Ready-mix is the practical choice for a retail pack that a customer opens and uses. Concentrate is the choice for a
distributor supplying refills, because you are not paying to ship water across an ocean — but you take on the mixing
consistency question, and inconsistent mixing produces inconsistent bubbles.

## The three failure modes customers report

1. **Bubbles pop instantly.** Usually a weak surfactant load or a dilution that is too thin. The machine is blamed.
2. **The wand does not pick up solution.** Often a viscosity problem — the solution is too thick and clings instead of
   forming a film.
3. **The machine clogs or foams over.** Over-concentration, or a reservoir that was topped up without cleaning.

Only the third is really a machine problem, and even that is usually an operating instruction problem.

## Why this is a margin question

Refill solution is a repeat purchase with a better margin than the toy, because it has no moulding cost and the customer
already owns the machine. A distributor who supplies the machine and the refill has a relationship; one who supplies only
the machine has a transaction.

That said, the refill only sells if the original solution was good. A customer whose first bottle produced weak bubbles
does not come back for a second.

## What to check on a sample

- Blow bubbles indoors, with no wind. If they cannot survive a slow float across a room, the solution is thin.
- Let a bubble land on a dark surface and watch how long it takes to pop.
- Run a machine for five minutes and look at the reservoir: is it foaming, or is the level dropping as it should?
- Leave a bottle overnight and check for separation or sediment.

## Shipping considerations

Solution is a liquid. That means a leak risk in a carton, and for air freight a possible restriction depending on the
formulation and the carrier. A leaking bottle inside a retail box damages the artwork of every unit near it, which is why
the bottle is normally sealed separately inside the box rather than loose.

If you are shipping by air, tell the supplier at the quote stage rather than at booking. Some carriers will not take
liquid-filled consumer products without documentation, and discovering that at the airport is the expensive version of
the conversation.

## The practical recommendation

When you compare bubble toys, compare them with the solution they ship with. Two machines at the same FOB price are not
the same product if one includes a 100 ml bottle of thin solution and the other a 250 ml bottle that actually works. Ask
for the volume, the dilution ratio if it is concentrate, and whether refill packs can be supplied — that third question
tells you whether the supplier thinks of the category as a consumable business or just as a box to ship.
