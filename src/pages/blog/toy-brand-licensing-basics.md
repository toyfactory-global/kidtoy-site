**Short answer:** if a toy uses a character, a logo, a team badge or a recognisable design that someone owns, someone needs a licence. The importer carries the commercial risk, not the factory — a seizure at the border is your container, and a takedown is your listing.

## How the risk actually arrives

Three ways, in increasing order of cost:

1. **A marketplace takedown.** A rights holder files a complaint, the listing goes, and the inventory becomes hard to sell.
2. **A customs seizure.** Border authorities in the EU and US act on recorded rights. Goods can be detained and destroyed, and the importer pays.
3. **A claim.** The rights holder pursues damages, which can exceed the value of the goods by a wide margin.

None of these requires the factory to have done anything dishonest. A generic mould with a character sticker on the box is enough.

## What people mean by "generic"

A generic toy uses an original design with no protected character, no team badge, no recognisable trade dress. A cartoon-style bubble gun is generic. The same gun with a well-known character printed on it is not, even if the print is small and the toy is cheap.

There is a middle zone that catches buyers: shapes that resemble a protected character closely enough to be a derivative work. "It doesn't say the name" is not a defence.

## What a licence grants, and what it does not

A licence typically grants the right to manufacture or sell specific products, in specific territories, for a specific term, at a royalty rate, with approvals on artwork and quality. It usually does not grant:

- The right to use the brand in your company name.
- The right to extend to other product categories.
- The right to sell outside the territory, including online sales that ship elsewhere.

That last point is where ecommerce sellers get caught: a licence for one country plus a website that ships worldwide is a mismatch.

## What to ask a supplier

- Is any part of this design licensed, and to whom?
- Does the artwork contain a trademark, a character or a badge?
- Can you supply the design as generic, without the licensed element?

A factory that runs generic moulds will usually say so plainly. A factory that offers "the same as the famous one" is offering a liability.

## What to do if you want a character

Approach the rights holder or their licensing agent before the product, not after. Licensing timelines are long — approvals, samples, artwork sign-off — and they are not compatible with a seasonal deadline you have already committed to.

The realistic path for a first range is generic: good mechanism, good packaging, own brand. Characters come later, with a licence, and usually with a partner who already holds one.

## The honest position

Our catalogue is generic by design. We do not print third-party characters on our products or packaging, and we will not do so on request without a licence document from the rights holder. That is not caution for its own sake — it is the difference between a product you can sell for years and a container you cannot clear.
