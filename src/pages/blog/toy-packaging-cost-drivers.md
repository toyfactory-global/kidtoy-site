**Short answer:** packaging cost is driven by the printing method, the number of colours, the board grade and — above all — the minimum run. On a first order the minimum matters more than the unit price, because a custom box can require several times the toy quantity.

## The four options

| Option | Unit cost | Minimum | Look | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Poly bag + header card | Lowest | Very low | Cheap | Multipacks, prize stock |
| Stock colour box + sticker | Low | At the toy MOQ | Neutral | Testing a listing, first order |
| Printed sleeve over stock box | Moderate | Moderate print run | Near-custom | Small brands, seasonal |
| Fully printed box | Highest | Highest print run | Custom | Established brand, retail shelf |

The jump from sleeve to fully printed box is where most overspend happens. A die-cut printed box needs its own print run, its own die and its own setup, and those costs are per design — not per unit — so a small order carries them all.

## Colours and finishes

Print cost rises with the number of colours and with each finishing step: matt or gloss lamination, spot UV, foil, embossing. A single-colour design on kraft board can be dramatically cheaper than a four-colour process box with matt lamination, and on a low price point the cheaper option often sells better because the price is lower.

Finish also affects durability in transit. Matt lamination scuffs; a varnish scuffs less but looks less premium; an uncoated board marks easily but photographs honestly.

## Board grade is a freight decision

A heavier board protects better and weighs more. On a volume-limited container the weight rarely binds, so a heavier board is usually cheap insurance — but it also makes the box larger for the same product if the flutes are deeper, and carton dimensions set your freight.

The practical test: pack one carton in the proposed packaging, drop it, and measure. If the box dimensions grow by 2 cm in one direction, the CBM grows by roughly that proportion, and so does the share of the container the order occupies.

## Inner packaging, which nobody quotes

The inner tray, the blister, the bag and the divider decide your damage rate. A unit that rattles in a box arrives with a scuffed face; a unit held in a moulded tray arrives intact but costs 5–10% more per unit.

For a printed retail box, inner packaging is usually worth the money — the box is what the customer sees, and a damaged box is an unsaleable unit. For a poly bag sold as a multipack, it usually is not.

## The three ways buyers overspend

1. **Custom box on an unproven product.** The print minimum commits more capital than the toy does, before the market has said anything.
2. **Printing a claim that has to change.** A box that says "CE certified" for every market will need a reprint when the requirement differs.
3. **Ignoring the carton dimension when choosing the box.** A slightly deeper box can push a shipment from a comfortable 40HQ fill to an awkward one.

## What to ask for

- The unit cost at your quantity, for each of the four options.
- The print minimum for a fully printed box, separately from the toy minimum.
- The carton dimension and CBM with the proposed packaging, not with the bare product.
- A physical sample of the box, not a PDF dieline.

That last one matters: a dieline shows the shape, not the board, the print quality or how the box closes.
