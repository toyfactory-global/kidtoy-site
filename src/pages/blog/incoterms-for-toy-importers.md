**Short answer:** FOB means the supplier gets the goods to the port and clears export; you pay the ocean and everything
after it. CIF means the supplier also pays freight and insurance to your port, but you still pay duty and clearance. DDP
means the supplier delivers duty paid to your door — convenient, opaque, and usually the most expensive per unit.

## The cost stack, term by term

| Cost | EXW | FOB | CIF | DDP |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Goods | You | You | You | Bundled |
| Inland haul to port (China) | You | Supplier | Supplier | Supplier |
| Export clearance | You | Supplier | Supplier | Supplier |
| Ocean / air freight | You | You | Supplier | Supplier |
| Marine insurance | You | You | Supplier | Supplier |
| Import duty | You | You | You | Supplier |
| Destination clearance | You | You | You | Supplier |
| Inland delivery to your door | You | You | You | Supplier |
| Risk transfers at | Factory | On board | On board | Your door |

Two things this table makes obvious. First, **CIF is not "delivered"** — it stops at your port, and the duty and clearance
are still yours. Second, **DDP bundles the duty into a number you cannot audit**, which is why it is comfortable and
expensive at the same time.

## Where risk actually moves

The Incoterm sets the point at which the goods become your problem. On FOB and CIF the risk passes when the goods are on
board the vessel, even though on CIF the supplier is still paying the freight. That gap matters: if the container is lost
at sea on a CIF shipment, the supplier has paid for a shipment that is now your loss, and you claim on the insurance —
which is why CIF quotes normally include a marine policy, and why you should check the insured value.

## The comparison mistake

A buyer receives an EXW quote of $2.20 and an FOB quote of $2.40 and concludes the EXW supplier is 8% cheaper. It is not:
EXW leaves you to arrange and pay the inland haul to the port and the export clearance, which on a small shipment can add
more than the $0.20 gap, and it leaves you responsible for a step you have probably never arranged before.

The fair comparison is: convert everything to the same term, then add your own freight and duty. That is what the
[landed cost calculator](/tools/landed-cost-calculator/) is for — put the FOB price in, put your own freight and duty in,
and compare landed cost per unit rather than headline price.

## When DDP is the right choice

DDP is genuinely useful in three situations:

- **You are testing a market** and do not want to set up an importer of record yet.
- **The order is small** and the fixed cost of a customs broker and a clearance file is disproportionate.
- **You are selling through a marketplace** that needs the goods in a domestic warehouse with the duty already accounted
  for.

It is a bad choice when the order is large enough that the duty is a material number, because you lose visibility of what
you are paying and you lose the ability to plan around preferential rates or a different tariff line.

## The question that reveals a fake DDP

Ask: "which HS line will you declare on the DDP shipment, and what duty rate does that line carry in my country?"

A supplier who can answer knows what they are doing. A supplier who cannot is either guessing the duty (and will recover
the difference later, or ship it wrong) or is not actually offering DDP but CIF with a courier label.

## Payment terms are a separate axis

Trade terms and payment terms get confused constantly. FOB/CIF/DDP describe who arranges and pays for transport. Payment
terms describe when money moves — deposit and balance against documents, letter of credit, or open account after a
history. A 30% deposit with 70% against the bill of lading is the usual structure for a first toy order; it is not a
consequence of choosing FOB.

## Practical guidance

For a first import, FOB is the default for a reason: it is the most comparable term, it puts the freight decision in your
hands where you can shop it, and it makes the supplier's responsibilities unambiguous. Use CIF only if you cannot get a
freight quote yourself, and DDP only for small or exploratory shipments.

Either way, get the carton data first. No trade term tells you what the goods will cost to move — that comes from the CBM,
which comes from the quotation, which is why the [quotation guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-toy-factory-quotation/) comes
before this one.
