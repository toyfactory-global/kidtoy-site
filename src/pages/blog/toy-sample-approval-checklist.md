**Short answer:** approve a sample against a written checklist, not against a feeling. Colour, material, dimensions,
function, packaging, labelling and battery behaviour all have to be checked and recorded, because the approved sample
becomes the only document that settles a dispute at inspection.

## Why the sample matters more than the contract

A purchase contract describes a product in words. Words are ambiguous: "pink" has a range, "strong" has a range, "same as
before" has a history you may not share. The approved sample is the physical object both sides agreed on, and at
pre-shipment inspection the question is almost always "does production match the approved sample", not "does it match the
description".

That is why a sloppy approval is expensive. If you approve a sample without checking the packing, you have approved the
packing. If you approve a colour under office lighting, you have approved that colour.

## The checklist

**Appearance**
- Colour against your reference — ideally in daylight, not under an office tube.
- Surface finish: any flow marks, sink marks or scuffs on the visible faces.
- Print and logo position, size and sharpness, including on curved surfaces.

**Material and construction**
- Material as specified, not a substitution. Ask for the material name, not "plastic".
- Wall thickness on load-bearing parts, especially handles and tank fittings.
- Screw bosses and clips: does anything feel loose on a new unit?

**Function**
- Run it for longer than feels necessary. A bubble machine that works for thirty seconds can still seize at twenty
  minutes.
- Battery compartment: does the cover seat, and does the seal actually contact?
- Switches and triggers: positive action, no rattle.

**Dimensions and weight**
- Measure the product, the retail box and the carton. Compare all three against the quotation, because the carton is what
  you pay freight on.

**Packaging and labelling**
- Retail box artwork, including the warning panel and the barcode position.
- Age grading, importer details and country of origin where the market requires them.
- Inner packing: is the unit protected, and does it survive a drop?

**Documentation**
- Which test reports exist for this item, with dates and the laboratory.
- For a battery item: the UN 38.3 status of the cell.

## The five things buyers forget

1. **The carton.** Everyone checks the toy. Almost nobody checks whether the carton is what was quoted. It is the single
   most expensive omission, because carton dimensions set your freight.
2. **A drop test.** Drop the retail box from counter height onto a hard floor. If the corner crushes, it will crush in a
   container.
3. **The battery under load.** A fresh alkaline cell hides a weak motor. Run it until the cell is half flat and see
   whether the function still works.
4. **The label text.** Read the warning panel aloud. Missing words and broken sentences in the artwork are extremely
   common and they are a compliance problem, not a typographic one.
5. **The second unit.** Ask for two samples of the same item. Variation between two samples tells you how much variation
   you will get across 1,000.

## What "approval" should look like in writing

Send an email that says what you approved, not just "approved". Something like:

> We approve sample received on [date] for item [SKU], with the following conditions: colour to match the sample,
> carton 53 × 45 × 59.5 cm with 96 pcs, retail box artwork as per the PDF dated [date], and the warning panel corrected to
> read [text]. Any change to these points must be confirmed with us in writing before production.

That paragraph is worth more than a signed form, because it names the conditions. When production differs, the argument is
about a specific sentence rather than about impressions.

## Where the process usually breaks

The break is almost never the factory. It is the buyer approving a sample in a hurry because a deadline is close, and
then discovering at inspection that the packing is different from what they assumed. The second most common break is a
change requested after approval — a colour tweak, a logo move — that never gets written down and therefore never gets
carried into production correctly.

If a change is needed after approval, ask for a revised sample or a written confirmation with a photo. It costs a few
days. Not doing it costs a container.
