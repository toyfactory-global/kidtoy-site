**Short answer:** each channel takes a different margin, and that margin is charged on the retail price. Work backwards
from the shelf price your channel can sustain, subtract the channel's margin and the landed-cost uplift, and you get the
FOB price you can afford. Doing it the other way round — starting from what the factory quoted — is how buyers end up with
a product that cannot be priced.

## The chain, in the right order

Shelf price → retailer margin → your margin → landed cost → FOB.

Most buyers start at the left and stop at "can I get it cheaper". The useful question is the reverse: what FOB price does
this shelf price actually support?

## Typical margin structures

| Channel | Retailer margin | Your margin needed | What this means for FOB |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gift shop / boutique | High | Moderate | Can carry a premium product |
| Toy specialist | Moderate | Moderate | Wants a range, prices competitively |
| Pharmacy / drugstore | Moderate | Low–moderate | Tight, volume-driven |
| Marketplace | Fees + fulfilment instead of margin | Moderate | Cheap units need multipacks |
| Discount chain | Very low | Very low | Volume only, no premium packaging |
| Party supplies | Moderate | Low | Carton economics matter more than margin |

Treat these as shapes rather than numbers. The margin a specific retailer takes is a negotiation between you and them, and
it varies by country and by category.

## Worked backwards

Suppose a gift shop can sell a makeup case at $19.99 and works on a 50% margin. That puts your wholesale price at about
$10. If the landed cost is 35% above FOB, your maximum FOB is roughly $7.40. Now look at the catalogue: cases at $6.42 to
$8.75 FOB fit that band, and the $12.69 to $23.03 formats do not.

That is the whole exercise, and it is why a supplier's price list is the last thing you should look at rather than the
first.

## The multipack adjustment

On a marketplace, a single low-value unit rarely works: platform fees and fulfilment are charged per order, not per
dollar. A $2.65 bubble machine sold alone at $9.99 leaves very little after fees, fulfilment and advertising. The same
unit in a three-pack at $17.99 changes the arithmetic completely, because the fixed costs are spread across three units.

This is the structural reason so much cheap toy volume online is sold in multipacks, and it should influence what you
order rather than how you price it afterwards.

## Where the price point breaks

Three common breakages:

1. **Packaging costs more than the price point allows.** A printed box on a $3 retail item destroys the margin. Use a
   sleeve, a sticker or stock packaging.
2. **Freight is treated as a percentage instead of a fixed cost.** On small orders the fixed charges dominate, and a
   channel that works at 1,000 pieces may not work at 200.
3. **The range has no price ladder.** Four items at the same price compete with each other. Three price points sell better
   than four near-duplicates.

## Setting the range, not the product

The practical output of all this is a ladder: an entry item, a core item, and a premium item, each with a clear reason to
exist. The entry item brings traffic, the core item makes the margin, the premium item gives the shelf a top end.

In our catalogue that ladder naturally exists in the makeup range — $5.72 box at the bottom, $7–$9 cases in the middle,
$12–$23 premium formats at the top — which is why the case format matters more than the contents when you are building a
range rather than buying a product.

## The calculation to run before the order

1. Shelf price your channel can sustain.
2. Subtract the retailer margin.
3. Subtract the landed uplift (freight, duty, fees).
4. That is your maximum FOB.
5. Compare with the catalogue. If nothing fits, the price point is wrong, not the product.
