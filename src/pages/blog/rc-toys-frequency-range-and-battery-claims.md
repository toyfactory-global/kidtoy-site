**Short answer:** on a remote-control toy, the numbers that matter are frequency band, number of actuators, battery capacity in mAh at a stated voltage, and charge time. Range is the number most often inflated, because it is measured outdoors with nothing in the way.

## Frequency: 2.4 GHz versus the old bands

Legacy RC toys run on 27 MHz or 40 MHz with a crystal pair — one channel per toy. Two of the same toy in one room interfere, which is why toy fairs and playrooms are full of abandoned cars.

**2.4 GHz with frequency hopping** solves that: the handset and receiver negotiate a channel and hop, so a dozen units can run side by side. Almost every current RC toy at trade level is 2.4 GHz, and it should be stated on the box.

Two practical consequences:

- **Pairing.** A 2.4 GHz set has a bind procedure — usually switch on the toy, then hold the handset button. If that procedure is not in the instructions in the buyer's language, you get support tickets, not returns, but they cost the same in time.
- **Interference at events.** In a hall with hundreds of units powered on, some hopping sets still step on each other. If you sell to event operators, ask the factory to test a batch of twenty together.

## Range claims

"30 metres" and "50 metres" are measured line of sight, outdoors, handset held clear of the body, with a fresh battery. Indoors, through a wall or around a corner, expect roughly half.

That is not dishonesty — it is the standard test condition. What you should do is quote range conservatively in your own listing. A buyer who gets 12 m from a "30 m" toy is annoyed; a buyer who gets 20 m from a "15 m" toy is pleased.

If a factory cannot tell you how range was measured, treat the number as decorative.

## Counting the actuators, not the "functions"

Marketing copy talks about functions. Engineering counts actuators — each motor, each servo, each solenoid.

A dinosaur that walks forward and backward has one motor and a reversing circuit. A robot with two arms, a rotating turret and a walking gait has three or four. Four actuators is roughly four times the mechanical failure surface of one, and it changes the price far more than piece count does.

When comparing two quotes, ask: how many motors, what type, and what is the gearbox material?

## Battery: mAh is half the story

Capacity in mAh tells you how much charge is in the cell. Runtime depends on the load, so a 500 mAh pack driving one motor can outlast a 1200 mAh pack driving four.

Ask for:

- **Cell configuration** — a single 3.7 V lithium cell, or a 2S pack at 7.4 V. The voltage decides the motor and the charger.
- **Charge time** and charge method (USB, dock, removable pack).
- **Stated runtime under load**, and whether it was measured with the model moving or idling.
- **Protection board** — over-charge, over-discharge and short circuit. On a cheap pack this is where cost gets cut, and it is the part that fails dangerously.

Removable packs are worth the extra cost for repeat buyers: the toy is not dead when the cell degrades.

## What actually fails in the field

Across returns on RC toys, the pattern is consistent and rarely electronic:

1. **Gearbox teeth.** Plastic gears strip when a child pushes the model backwards while the motor is driving forwards.
2. **Charging port.** Micro-USB sockets pull off the board when a cable is yanked. A recessed port with strain relief is a cheap fix.
3. **Battery swelling.** Usually a storage problem — a pack left flat for months in a hot warehouse.
4. **Wheel and joint pins.** Friction fit fails; a screw or a clip costs almost nothing.
5. **Transmitter sticks.** The potentiometers wear before the radio does.

None of these show up in a ten-minute sample check. They show up in your first hundred units. Ask a factory what their return rate is on the model, and which part accounts for most of it — the answer tells you whether they track it at all.

## The document set that travels with it

An RC toy with a lithium pack is a battery product as well as a toy. Expect a UN 38.3 test summary for the cell, and air freight restrictions that do not apply to a manual toy. That paperwork is not optional and it is not the same for every shipping method.

## What to ask before ordering

1. Frequency band and whether the handset binds or pairs.
2. Number of motors, type, and gearbox material.
3. Battery chemistry, voltage, capacity, and whether there is a protection board.
4. Stated range and the conditions it was measured under.
5. Spare parts availability — packs, gears, transmitters.
6. Battery documentation for the shipping method you intend to use.

A remote-control toy is bought on the demo and reordered on reliability. The specifications above are how you tell in advance which one you are getting.
