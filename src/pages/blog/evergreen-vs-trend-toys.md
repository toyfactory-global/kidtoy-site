**Short answer:** evergreen lines pay the bills and trend lines win the season, but trend stock that does not sell is
worth close to nothing the following year. The split depends on whether the item has a mould that will still exist, a
demand curve you can read, and a price point that survives being discounted.

## What "evergreen" actually means

Not "boring". A bubble gun is evergreen because the underlying activity — children chasing bubbles outdoors — is not
fashionable, it is permanent. A licensed character bubble gun is a trend item, even though the mechanism is identical,
because the demand is tied to a property with a release schedule.

That distinction is more useful than "trendy or classic", because it tells you what can go wrong. An evergreen item can go
out of stock; a trend item can go out of relevance, which is worse.

## The risk profile

| | Evergreen | Trend |
|---|---|---|
| Demand shape | Seasonal but repeatable | Spiky, dated |
| Leftover stock | Sells next season | Often unsaleable at full price |
| Reorder | Straightforward | Race against the curve |
| Price pressure | Steady, competitive | Collapses at the end |
| Mould life | Long | Often short |
| Best use | Base load | Top-up and opportunity |

## Reading a mould's life

Ask a supplier a direct question: is this mould likely to be in production in two years? A mould with a long run, used
across several colourways and several customers, is likely to persist. A mould made for one buyer's single season is not.

This matters more than the price. An evergreen line you can reorder in eighteen months is an asset; a line that vanishes
leaves you with a listing you cannot restock and customers you cannot serve.

## How to split the budget

There is no universal ratio, but there is a useful discipline: fund the base load with the lines you have already sold,
and fund trend items only from margin you can afford to lose. A buyer who puts 70% of a season's budget into an unproven
trend is not taking a risk, they are making a bet with the whole business.

The practical structure many of our buyers use:

- **60–70% evergreen** — proven lines, reordered, with the carton plan already worked out.
- **20–30% seasonal** — themed packaging or themed items on a proven mechanism, ordered to a specific window.
- **10% experiment** — one or two new items at MOQ, tested and then either scaled or dropped.

## The discount trap

Trend stock that misses its window gets discounted, and the discount is usually deeper than the margin. That is the
asymmetry: a trend item that sells makes a good margin, one that misses makes a loss, and the loss is not symmetrical with
the gain.

This is why seasonal buying should be planned backwards from the shelf date, and why the order should be sized to the
window rather than to the price tier.

## What suppliers can tell you that helps

A factory that has run a mould for years knows how the demand behaves. Ask:

- How long has this item been in production?
- What colours or packaging variants does it run in?
- Is it a stock item or made to order?
- What is the usual reorder interval for this item?

Those four answers tell you more about durability than any trend report, because they are drawn from production history
rather than from a forecast.

## The conclusion

Buy the mechanism, not the character. Mechanisms are evergreen and moulds persist; characters and gimmicks are rented for a
season. When you do buy a trend item, size it as an experiment and plan the exit before you place the order.
