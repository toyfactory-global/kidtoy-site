**Short answer:** if you want a product nobody else can sell, you need OEM and you need to pay for tooling. If you want to test a category quickly with your branding on it, ODM gets you to market in weeks instead of months — but the design is not exclusively yours.

Most first-time toy brands start with ODM and move to OEM once a product proves itself. Starting with OEM on an unproven design is the expensive way to learn.

## Definitions, without the jargon

**OEM — Original Equipment Manufacturer.** In toy sourcing, this means the factory manufactures a product to your design or specification. You own (or commission) the tooling. The factory builds it.

**ODM — Original Design Manufacturer.** The factory already owns the design and the mould. You select it from their catalogue, apply your branding, and buy it. The design remains the factory's.

The confusing part: both are "factory direct" and both can carry your logo. The difference is **who owns the design**, and that determines everything downstream.

## Side by side

| | OEM | ODM |
|---|---|---|
| Design ownership | Yours (with tooling) | Factory's |
| Tooling cost | Paid by you, quoted per project | None — existing mould |
| MOQ | Usually higher — a new run on new tooling | Lower — often the standard carton multiple |
| Time to first shipment | Longer: sampling, tooling, T1/T2 samples | Short — sample, brand, pack |
| Exclusivity | Yours by default | Not exclusive unless negotiated |
| Changes mid-project | Possible but costly once tooling is cut | Limited to branding and packaging |
| Best for | Proving a unique product, building IP | Testing a category, seasonal ranges, private label |

## The cost you are really paying in OEM

Tooling is quoted per project, and the quote depends on cavity count, part complexity, surface finish and whether the mould is steel or aluminium. There is no useful generic number — anyone who quotes you one without seeing the part is guessing.

What you should understand is the structure:

- **Tooling is a one-time capital cost**, paid before production. It is not amortised into the unit price unless you negotiate that.
- **The mould belongs to whoever paid for it.** If you pay, get it in writing that the tooling is your property and can be transferred to another moulder.
- **A second colour or a variant usually needs a second tool** — or a changeover, which costs setup time on every run.

That last point is where OEM quotes surprise buyers. "Two colours" is not a packaging change; it can double the tooling line.

## The trap in ODM

ODM is fast and cheap, and the trade-off is that **the product is not yours**. Three scenarios follow from that:

1. **Your competitor can buy it.** Unless you negotiate exclusivity — and expect to pay for it with volume commitments — the same item is available to anyone the factory sells to.
2. **Your listing is not unique.** Two sellers listing the same ODM bubble gun compete on price, and the one with the lowest margin wins. That is a race you do not want to enter.
3. **Changes are cosmetic.** You can change the colour box, the manual, the logo and often the colour of the plastic. You cannot change the mechanism.

None of this makes ODM a bad choice. It makes it a **market-entry tool**, not a moat.

## Packaging is where ODM gets most of its value

The single highest-leverage change on an ODM product is the retail packaging. A colour box redesign changes how the product reads on a shelf or in a marketplace thumbnail, and it is typically the cheapest customisation available.

## A decision sequence that works

1. **Pick the category with ODM.** Buy a small run at the standard MOQ and test sell-through with your branding.
2. **Find the one thing customers complain about.** Reviews and returns tell you what to change.
3. **Move that one product to OEM.** Take the proven design, commission a mould with the fix built in, and now you own something.
4. **Keep the rest as ODM.** Not every SKU in a catalogue needs to be proprietary.

This sequence keeps your capital concentrated on the products that have already earned it.

## What to ask either way

- OEM: "What is the tooling cost, what is the cavity count, who owns the mould, and what is the minimum run after tooling?"
- ODM: "How many other buyers currently purchase this item, and what would exclusivity cost?"
- Both: "What is the carton quantity, the CBM, and the MOQ in cartons?"

The second question is uncomfortable to ask and highly informative to hear answered.
