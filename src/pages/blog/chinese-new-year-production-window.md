**Short answer:** plan for a production window that closes three to four weeks before the holiday and reopens at full speed four to five weeks after it. The official holiday is the shortest part of the disruption.

## What actually happens

The official public holiday runs for about a week. In a factory town like Chenghai, the real effect is longer and shaped differently:

- **Weeks before:** workers begin leaving for long journeys home, often before the official start. Lines lose people unevenly, so output falls before it stops.
- **During:** the site is quiet. Samples are not made, approvals do not happen, emails go unanswered.
- **Weeks after:** workers return gradually. A line that needs thirty people does not restart at thirty. Ramp-up is real production time but at reduced output, with higher defect rates while new or returning staff find the rhythm.
- **Sub-suppliers matter as much.** A box printer, a plater, a battery pack assembler keeps its own calendar. Your factory can be open while its carton supplier is not.

Add those together and a two-week holiday becomes a six-to-eight-week hole in the year.

## The cut-off that matters

The date to plan around is not the holiday start. It is the last date a container can be loaded and cleared before the port slows down.

Working backwards from a sailing date:

| Step | Typical lead |
|---|---|
| Port handling and customs at origin | A few days, longer in the pre-holiday peak |
| Inland transport to port | One to three days |
| Final inspection and packing | Several days |
| Production run | Depends on quantity and complexity |
| Material procurement | One to three weeks |

If you want goods to sail before the shutdown, the order usually has to be placed in the autumn, with materials booked early. Anything confirmed close to the holiday either ships late or ships after.

## The two windows worth knowing

**Before the holiday.** Everyone wants to ship in the weeks before the shutdown, so space tightens and rates rise. Booking early is worth more than negotiating a lower unit price and then paying peak freight.

**After the holiday.** The first containers out are typically the ones whose production finished before the break. Production started after the break lands weeks later. If your season depends on that window, it is already too late by the time the factory reopens.

## What to do differently

**Place the order earlier than feels necessary.** The cost of holding stock for a few extra weeks is usually lower than the cost of missing a season.

**Book freight when you confirm production, not when it is ready.** Peak capacity before the holiday is the constraint, and it is bought in advance.

**Approve samples before the shutdown.** A sample approval that lands in the middle of the holiday stalls the whole order by a month. This is the single most common planning failure we see.

**Split the order deliberately.** Ship a first tranche on the pre-holiday vessels, and let the balance follow on the post-holiday ramp-up. You sell something early instead of everything late.

**Ask the factory what their own sub-suppliers do.** A factory that knows its carton printer closes two weeks earlier than it does is a factory that can plan. One that does not is guessing.

## For the factory's side

From our own calendar in Chenghai: we publish a cut-off date for orders that need to ship before the holiday, and we stop accepting new production commitments for that window once the material lead time no longer fits. Buyers who ask for the cut-off date early get a real answer; buyers who ask in the last fortnight get the truth, which is that the window has closed.

The useful question is not "when do you close" but "what is the last date you can guarantee a container before you close".

## A simple annual rhythm

- **Late summer / early autumn:** plan the spring and summer range; place pre-holiday orders.
- **Autumn:** production and pre-holiday shipping.
- **Holiday period:** no new commitments; approvals paused.
- **Early in the new year:** post-holiday ramping; good time for development, sampling and tool work rather than volume.
- **Spring:** volume production for the second half.

Buyers who shift development and tooling into the quiet period get their samples approved with no queue, and their production scheduled into the front of the post-holiday ramp-up.

## The one-line version

Treat the shutdown as a six-week gap in the year with a hard cut-off in front of it, and the calendar stops being a surprise. Treat it as a two-week holiday, and every year you will be explaining a delay that everyone could see coming.
