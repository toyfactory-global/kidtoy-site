**Short answer:** in a tabletop game, the board and the box are the fixed cost, the cards and moulded components are the variable cost, and the insert tray is the line item people forget. Rules cost nothing to print and everything to get wrong — a badly written rulebook generates more support email than a chipped token.

## Component by component

### The board

A folding board is greyboard wrapped in printed paper. Two numbers decide the price:

- **Greyboard thickness.** Thin board warps when humidity changes between the printing plant and the destination port. Thicker board is heavier and costs more to ship. For a game that will sit on a shelf, thickness is worth paying for; for a game that ships in a mailer, weight matters more.
- **Print coverage.** A board printed edge to edge uses more ink and needs better registration than a mostly-white board.

Ask for the board's unfolded and folded dimensions and its thickness in millimetres. "Standard board" is not a specification.

### Cards

Card price is driven by four things: quantity, stock weight, core colour and finish.

- **Stock weight** — heavier card feels better and shuffles better; it also costs more per deck.
- **Core colour** — a black-core card hides edge wear; a cheaper grey or white core shows white edges after a few plays.
- **Finish** — linen or air-cushion finish reduces sticking; a plain varnish is cheaper and cards clump.
- **Count** — this is where MOQ economics bite. A 54-card deck and a 110-card deck can cost almost the same to set up and print, because the setup dominates at low volume.

If your game needs 40 cards, print 54 and use the extras for variants. You are paying for the sheet either way.

### Moulded components

Plastic tokens, dice, standees and miniatures are injection parts, so they carry tooling. A token shared across your range amortises; a unique miniature does not.

For small quantities, printed cardboard punch-outs are usually cheaper than moulded plastic — no tool, and the die is reusable. The trade-off is feel and durability, and buyers notice both.

### The insert

The vacuum-formed or folded tray that holds everything is the component most often cut in cost negotiations. Cut it badly and components rattle, cards bend, and the game arrives looking used.

A simple folded cardboard insert is sufficient for a light game. A deep game with miniatures needs a moulded tray, and that tray needs its own tool.

### The box and the shrink

Rigid box versus folding carton is a step change in cost, not a percentage. A magnetic-closure rigid box signals a premium game; a folding carton with good print can look almost as good on a shelf at a fraction of the price.

## Language versions

This is the decision that has the largest effect on cost per market, and it is usually made too late.

Artwork separates into **text-free components** (board art, tokens, components) and **language components** (rules, cards with words, box copy). If you design for that split from the start:

- One print run of boards serves every language.
- Cards need a separate run per language, so the card MOQ applies per language.
- Rules booklets are cheap and short-run friendly.

If you do not design for it, you reprint everything per market and pay full setup each time.

## MOQ economics

Print setup does not scale down. The practical consequence:

| Quantity | What changes |
|---|---|
| Very low (hundreds) | Setup dominates; consider digital print and a simpler spec |
| Low thousands | Offset becomes viable; board and card specs can be upgraded cheaply |
| High thousands | Component cost dominates; tooling amortises; the box becomes the lever |

The mistake at the low end is specifying a premium board and a rigid box, then discovering the unit cost is double the target. At that volume, spend on the artwork and the rules, not the substrate.

## Quality points that come back to you

- **Board warp.** Check a sample after a week in normal room humidity, not on the day it arrives.
- **Card registration.** Misaligned backs give away the deck.
- **Insert fit.** If a component shifts in transit, it arrives damaged.
- **Rule clarity.** Play the game from the rulebook alone, with someone who has not seen it before. This is the only test that matters and almost nobody runs it.

## What to ask before you commit

1. Board thickness, folded size, and whether it is greyboard or corrugated.
2. Card count, stock weight, core colour, finish.
3. Which components are moulded, and whether the tool already exists.
4. Insert type and whether it has its own tool.
5. Price per language version, and whether artwork is already split.
6. A sample played from the rules by someone outside the project.

A tabletop game is a print job with a rules problem attached. Price the print first, then decide whether the game deserves the better board.
