**Short answer:** a first order is normally a deposit with production, then the balance against shipping documents — commonly 30% down and 70% before the bill of lading is released. That structure is standard, and it is also the point at which most first-time buyers have the least protection.

## The three structures you will meet

**Deposit and balance (T/T).** The most common. The deposit funds materials, the balance is paid when the goods are ready and the documents exist. The buyer's protection comes from the pre-shipment inspection happening before the balance moves.

**Letter of credit.** A bank guarantees payment against specified documents. Expensive to set up and administratively heavy, but it is the tool for orders large enough that the deposit risk matters more than the bank fees.

**Open account.** Goods ship, payment follows in 30, 60 or 90 days. Only on a real relationship, and usually only after several completed orders. Any new supplier offering open account on a first order is either desperate or not telling you something.

## Why the deposit exists

A toy order commits money before it produces anything: resin, components, printed packaging, and labour. A factory that starts production with nothing down is financing your inventory, and very few can.

That does not mean the deposit percentage is fixed. It moves with the item, the mould (new tooling usually needs more), and how long the relationship has run.

## What actually protects the buyer

Not the contract language — the inspection. The sequence that protects you is:

1. Approve a sample in writing, with the carton and artwork conditions named.
2. Pay the deposit against that approval, not before.
3. Inspect before the balance is due, using a third party if you can.
4. Pay the balance against documents, and check the bill of lading matches the shipment.

If a supplier wants the full balance before you have seen the finished goods, that is a commercial risk decision — not a normal term. It happens with new suppliers on small orders, and the honest response is to reduce the order size rather than to accept a process you cannot verify.

## Four warning signs

- **A bank account that changes mid-order.** Bank details change requests are a known fraud pattern. Confirm any change by a second channel, never by email alone.
- **A personal account** instead of a company account, on an order of any size.
- **Refusal to allow inspection** before the balance.
- **A price well below every other quote** combined with a demand for a large deposit — that is the shape of an order that will not ship as described.

## Currency and the exchange rate

Toy quotes are usually in US dollars. The factory carries the exchange risk between quotation and deposit, which is one reason a quote has a validity period. If a quote is months old and the rate has moved a long way, expect it to be re-quoted — that is not bad faith.

## What to put in writing

- Deposit percentage and when it is due.
- The balance trigger, named precisely (for example: against a copy of the bill of lading).
- Inspection rights and who pays for the inspector.
- The bank account, in the contract, with the account name matching the company name on the invoice.

If that does not suit your risk appetite, the honest alternatives are a smaller first order or a letter of credit — not a different promise from us.
