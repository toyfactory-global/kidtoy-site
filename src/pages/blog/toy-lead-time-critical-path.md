**Short answer:** the factory quotes production time, but the order also includes sampling, artwork, deposit transfer, inspection booking, booking a vessel and clearance. Those steps are most of the elapsed time, and half of them wait on the buyer rather than the factory.

## The path, in order

**1. Enquiry to quotation** — days, depends on how specific your question is. An item number and a quantity gets a fast answer; a vague description gets questions.

**2. Sample** — days for a stock item, longer for a colour change or printed box. Then it has to reach you, which is a courier leg.

**3. Sample approval** — this is the step that slips, because it waits on you and on anyone else in your business who has an opinion.

**4. Artwork** — a dieline, a proof, corrections, a final approval. A week if the copy is ready, three if it is being rewritten internally.

**5. Deposit** — a bank transfer, sometimes a compliance check at your end. Rarely long, occasionally surprising.

**6. Production** — 15–25 days for a standard order, longer for new tooling or a complex assembly.

**7. Inspection** — has to be booked against the production finish date, not requested after the goods are packed.

**8. Booking and loading** — vessel schedules are weekly on many lanes; miss one and you wait seven days.

**9. Transit, clearance, inland** — 20–35 days at sea on most toy lanes, plus clearance and delivery.

## Where the time actually goes

Production is a quarter of the elapsed time, at most. The rest is sampling, approval, artwork, booking and transit — and the approval and artwork steps are the ones that expand when nobody is pushing them.

The practical consequence: a buyer who starts twelve weeks out and moves quickly succeeds. A buyer who starts twelve weeks out and takes two weeks to approve a sample does not.

## What the buyer controls

- How specific the initial enquiry is.
- How fast the sample is approved, and whether the approver is available.
- Whether the artwork is ready before the factory asks for it.
- Whether the inspection is booked early.
- Whether the vessel is booked as soon as the ready date is known.

Every one of those is a delay that looks like a factory problem and is not.

## What the factory controls

- The accuracy of the production window it quotes.
- Whether it tells you about a problem early or at the end.
- Whether it holds the ship date when a material is late.

Ask for the ready date and treat it as the number to manage. If it moves, you want to know at the moment it moves, not at inspection.

## Planning for a seasonal deadline

Count backwards: shelf date, minus clearance and inland (about a week), minus transit (three to five weeks), minus booking buffer (a week), minus production (three to four weeks), minus sample and artwork (two to three weeks). That total is the date the order has to start.

If that date has already passed, the honest options are air freight, a smaller first shipment, or a different product. Not a promise that production can be compressed — a toy that is rushed is a toy that fails inspection.

## The single most useful habit

Put the ready date, the inspection booking and the vessel booking on one calendar at the moment you place the order, with the owner of each step named. Almost every late shipment we see has a date that nobody was watching, rather than a factory that could not produce.
