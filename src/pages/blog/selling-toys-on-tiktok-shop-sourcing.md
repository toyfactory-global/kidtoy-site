**Short answer:** short-video commerce sells a demonstration, not a specification. The product has to do something visible
in the first two seconds, survive single-unit fulfilment, and be cheap enough that a return does not wipe out the sale.

## What changes when video sells the product

In a search-driven listing the buyer compares specifications. In a video feed the buyer sees a result and wants it. That
flips the sourcing criteria:

- **Visible effect beats features.** A stream of bubbles reads instantly; a "dual-mode induction sensor" does not.
- **Size on screen beats size in hand.** A product that looks substantial on camera but is light to ship has an economic
  advantage — as long as it does not disappoint on arrival.
- **Sound matters.** A toy with music or a motor noise performs differently on video than in silence.
- **The first frame matters.** Anything that requires setup before it does something is a harder sell.

## The fulfilment reality

Single-unit fulfilment is the cost centre. Whether you use a platform fulfilment service or your own 3PL, every order
carries a fixed picking, packing and shipping cost that does not shrink for a cheap item.

That is why the same product sells better as a bundle. A single $3 bubble gun at $12 retail leaves very little after
fulfilment and platform fees; a two-pack at $19 or a kit with solution and spare batteries does much better. If you are
sourcing for video commerce, source for a bundle from the start — ask the factory for the carton and the unit dimensions
with the bundle in mind.

## Returns are the hidden cost

Toy returns in social commerce are driven by expectation mismatch, not by defects. The video showed a huge bubble; the
product makes small ones in a breeze. The buyer returns it, and the return shipping on a low-value item can exceed the
product cost.

Three sourcing responses:

1. **Film the real thing.** Whatever you shoot should be the actual unit, not a competitor's footage.
2. **Choose demonstrations that are reproducible.** Indoor-friendly, low-wind, no-assembly products return less.
3. **State the battery situation.** "Batteries not included" in the first line of the description prevents a whole
   category of complaints.

## Packaging that survives a courier

A retail box in a poly mailer arrives with crushed corners. For video commerce, the outer packaging is part of the
product experience — unboxing footage is content, and a damaged box is a negative post.

Ask for a mailer-ready carton or a shipping box, and check the drop test at the sample stage. It is a small cost line that
protects both the product and the content.

## What to look for in a supplier

- Stock available in the quantity you can actually sell, not a 500-piece MOQ for a test.
- Fast reorder, because a video can spike and then die within weeks.
- Consistent production, because a second batch that looks different ruins the footage you already published.
- A product that photographs well under ordinary lighting, without a studio.

## The risk to price in

Short-video demand is spiky and short-lived. Buying 2,000 units of a product because one video worked is how sellers end
up with dead stock. The healthier pattern is a small first order, a fast reorder while the video is live, and a planned
exit before the trend fades.

That argues for a supplier who can turn a repeat order quickly rather than one who offers the lowest price at 5,000
pieces. Speed of replenishment is the competitive advantage in this channel, not unit cost.
