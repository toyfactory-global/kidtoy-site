**Short answer:** price is mostly fixed by the mould, the material and the packaging. If you want a lower number, change one of those five levers rather than asking for a discount — a request with no structural change gets either a refusal or a lower grade product.

## Lever 1: quantity

The strongest lever, and the easiest to use badly. Unit price falls with quantity because setup, moulding time and packing labour are spread further. But the gain per step shrinks, and past a container the price barely moves.

The mistake is chasing a price tier that requires holding stock for months. The saving on the goods is usually smaller than the cost of the inventory.

## Lever 2: packaging

A printed box costs more than a stock box plus a sticker. Switching to a sleeve, reducing colours, dropping a lamination or moving to a poly bag with a header card can cut more from the landed cost than any negotiation on the toy itself — because packaging is where the avoidable spend sits.

## Lever 3: specification

Simpler is cheaper, and it is usually not worse:

- One colour of plastic instead of two.
- Fewer moulded parts, fewer assembly steps.
- A standard battery configuration instead of a proprietary pack.
- Fewer painted surfaces, which also reduces the chemical screening burden.

The honest caveat: any spec change is a new product to test and a new sample to approve. Do not simplify after the sample is signed off unless you are willing to re-run that step.

## Lever 4: payment terms

A supplier financing materials for you carries a cost, and early or larger deposits can be worth a concession. This is a real lever on a first order and a marginal one on a repeat.

Be careful with the reverse trade: a supplier offering an unusually low price for a large upfront payment is asking you to carry the risk instead of them.

## Lever 5: carton efficiency

Not a price per unit at all — a price per landed unit. A carton 2 cm smaller is often worth more than a 3% discount, because freight is charged on volume and every carton in the container shares it.

This is the lever buyers ignore, and it is free. Ask for the carton dimensions on the quotation, then ask whether a different inner packing would reduce them.

## What does not work

**"Your competitor quoted less."** Without the carton, the MOQ and the packaging, the comparison is not a comparison. A serious supplier will ask for those three, which is a good sign.

**Asking for a discount with no change.** You will either get a no, or a yes that is recovered later in the material or the packing. A price that moves without a reason is a warning, not a win.

**Negotiating before the specification is settled.** Every change afterwards re-opens the number, and the second negotiation is always worse than the first.

## The order to do it in

1. Fix the item and the packaging.
2. Get the carton data.
3. Model the landed cost.
4. Only then discuss the price, with the quantity and the terms on the table.

Most buyers run this backwards, negotiate a unit price, and then discover that the freight per piece makes the whole order uneconomic. The number on the quotation is the least important number in the calculation.
