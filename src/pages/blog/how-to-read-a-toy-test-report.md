**Short answer:** read the scope before the results. A report with thirty pages of passes is worthless if the sample photographs show a different product, if the standard edition is superseded, or if the colourways and materials in your carton are not listed.

## The first page is the one that matters

Most of the risk sits in the administrative block, not in the result tables.

| What to check | Why |
|---|---|
| Applicant | Is it the manufacturer, or a trading company? |
| Sample description and item number | Does it match what you are buying? |
| Sample photographs | Does the photo show your product, in your colourway? |
| Standard and edition | Is the edition current, or superseded? |
| Age grade tested | Does it match the packaging? |
| Test dates | Is the report recent enough for your buyer? |
| Laboratory and accreditation | Is it accepted for these tests in the destination market? |

A report is a statement about a specific article on a specific date. Everything outside that boundary is an assumption.

## The scope section

Reports usually include a statement of which models or materials the result covers — often phrased as a "series" or "family".

Two things to verify:

1. **The product family is defined by something you can check.** Same mould and same materials is a defensible family. "Similar product" is not.
2. **Every material in your carton is listed.** A report covering the body plastic but not the printed decal, the paint, or the soft grip is missing a material, and the untested material is the one that fails.

If your product comes in three colours and only one was tested, the standard question is whether the pigment changes the material's test status. Sometimes it does not. The point is that you asked and have the answer in writing.

## Reading a migration result

Migration results are usually presented as a measured value against a limit, per element, per material.

What to look at:

- **Which material category was applied.** The limits differ by material type — dry and brittle, liquid or sticky, scraped-off. A soft TPE part and a rigid ABS part are assessed differently, and the lab should state which category it used.
- **How close the result is to the limit.** A result at a fraction of the limit is comfortable. A result close to the limit will fail on a different batch, because material batches vary. If you see a number near the ceiling, ask what the margin is and whether the next production run will be retested.
- **Whether "not detected" is reported as such**, with a detection limit, or simply omitted.

## The clauses buyers skip

Three clauses worth reading on any toy report:

**Small parts and torque/tension.** For anything with projections or parts that could detach. The report should identify which components were tested, not just say "pass".

**Sharp edges and points.** Usually assessed by inspection and by instrument. Ask whether it was done before or after the drop test — after is more meaningful.

**Flammability**, where applicable to the material. Plush and textiles are the usual scope; solid plastic often is not.

## What a report does not tell you

- **It does not cover production control.** A pass means the sample passed.
- **It does not cover a material change.** If the factory switches resin or paint after testing, the report describes a product that no longer exists.
- **It does not transfer to another factory.** Reports belong to the applicant and the sample; a report from one factory is not evidence for another factory's goods, even if the design looks the same.
- **It does not cover a different market.** A report against one market's standard is not automatically accepted in another, even when the requirements overlap.

## The three questions to ask a supplier

1. **Is this report for this item number, and can you show me the sample photos?**
2. **Which standard edition, and is it the current one?**
3. **If I change the colour or the material, do we retest, and who pays?**

The third question is the one that separates a supplier who understands compliance from one who treats it as paperwork. A supplier who says "no need to retest for a colour change" without checking is guessing.

## Filing it so it is usable later

- Store reports by item number, with the date in the filename.
- Keep the sample photographs alongside, because in two years the report will be unreadable without them.
- Record the production batch the tested sample came from.
- Note the expiry of the standard edition, so a revision does not surprise you mid-season.

A report is a single point of evidence in a chain. This article is about making sure the link is actually connected to your container.
