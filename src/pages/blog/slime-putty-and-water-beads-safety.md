**Short answer:** slime, putty and water beads are not really plastic toys — they are formulated products sold as toys. They are tested on what migrates out of the gel, they degrade on a shelf, and they are restricted on aircraft. Buyers who treat them like a moulded toy get caught on all three.

## Four product types, four risk profiles

**Slime.** A gel, usually based on PVA with a borax or boron cross-linker, plus colour and fragrance. The recurring test failure in this category is boron migration, not the base polymer.

**Putty.** Similar chemistry, firmer, often silicone-based. Silicone putty avoids the boron question but brings its own ingredient documentation.

**Water beads.** Superabsorbent polymer beads that swell many times their dry size in water. The hazard is the dry bead: small, attractive, and dangerous if swallowed because it grows.

**Hydrogel craft kits.** Bottles of gel and moulds, where the child extrudes shapes into water. Safer than loose beads, but the same chemistry questions apply to the gel.

## What the tests actually look at

For a gel product, the interesting parts of the test report are:

- **Migration from the gel mass** — what leaches out under the standard's simulated conditions, by element.
- **Boron content** — the cross-linker is the usual problem, and it is the reason cheap slime gets refused at retail.
- **Preservatives** — a water-based gel grows things. Preservative choice and level are a formulation issue, and some preservatives are restricted.
- **pH** — both for skin contact and because it affects the preservative's effectiveness.
- **Colourant and fragrance documentation** — the same standards apply as for any toy material.

A gel that passes on the day it is made can fail after three months in a warm warehouse, because water evaporates and concentrations rise. That is not a testing problem, it is a formulation and packaging problem.

## Shelf life and packaging

This is where the category costs money and where buyers get surprised.

- **Evaporation.** A poorly sealed tub loses water and the product thickens or dries. Ask for the closure tested on a filled tub, not an empty one.
- **Mould growth.** A gel with insufficient preservative grows visible mould in a warm container. Ask about accelerated ageing results, not just a fresh-batch test.
- **Leakage in transit.** A tub that leaks ruins the carton and every product around it. Individual bagging is not optional here.
- **Labelling.** Ingredient and hazard information has to be legible on a small tub; that drives label size, which drives packaging cost.

Ask for a stated shelf life and the conditions it assumes. "Two years" without a temperature assumption is not a number you can plan a season around.

## Age grading on water beads

Dry water beads are a small-part and ingestion hazard. They are also visually indistinguishable from candy to a small child. This is the single most important call in the category, and it belongs in the high age band — not 3+.

For hydrogel craft kits where the child extrudes shapes rather than handling loose beads, the risk profile is different, but the gel still needs the migration and preservative documentation.

If your market's current standard edition has specific requirements for expanding materials, confirm with your lab which edition applies to your product. Standards are revised, and a report against an older edition may not be accepted.

## Freight and storage

Two restrictions that catch people:

- **Liquids by air.** Gel products are liquids for air-freight purposes and are subject to restrictions that do not apply to a moulded toy. Expect paperwork, and expect some carriers to refuse.
- **Heat.** A container crossing a tropical route in summer reaches temperatures well above ambient. Gel in a sealed tub under those conditions can weep or separate. Ship earlier in the season, or specify a thicker tub.

## What to ask before ordering

1. Full formulation summary, including the cross-linker and the preservative system.
2. Test report clauses that cover migration from the gel, not just the packaging.
3. Preservative efficacy and accelerated ageing results.
4. Stated shelf life with the temperature it assumes.
5. Closure and bagging: how the filled tub is sealed.
6. Age grade, and the basis for it.
7. Whether the product can ship by air and under what documentation.

## Why this category is worth the effort

Gel and sensory products have repeat purchase behaviour that moulded toys do not, and they photograph extremely well. That combination is rare in toys. The price of entry is doing the formulation homework, and the cost of skipping it is a whole container that cannot be sold.
