**Short answer:** on a magic set, the props are cheap and the explanation is the product. A set with 22 props of which four work reliably will outsell a set with 30 props that half-work, because the buyer's first evening is the whole decision.

## The four props that carry a set

Every beginner magic set has a spine: a couple of self-working props, one prop that requires a sleight, and a finale. Around that spine sit filler items — coins, cups, a wand — that exist to raise the count on the box.

This matters commercially for one reason: **the filler is where returns come from.** A prop that does not work as described, or that the buyer cannot learn from the instructions, becomes a one-star review that mentions the whole set.

When you evaluate a sample, sort the props into three groups:

| Group | Test | Commercial meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Self-working | Does it work in an untrained hand, first time | The set's floor; must be reliable |
| Learnable | Can an adult follow the instructions in 15 minutes | The set's ceiling |
| Filler | Is it a duplicate of another prop, or trivial | Cut it and cut the box claim |

A set presented honestly as "18 props, 12 self-working" is stronger than "30 props" that disappoints.

## Where the cost sits

**Props.** Most are injection-moulded or stamped metal. Per unit they are cheap; the tooling is what costs. A stock prop that the factory already moulds costs almost nothing to add; a new prop is a new tool.

**Printed instructions.** A multi-page booklet in colour, translated, is a real cost — and it is the one line item buyers try to cut first. Do not. A one-sheet instruction set on a 22-prop kit is the fastest way to generate support email.

**Packaging with compartments.** A magic set needs a tray so props do not rattle. That tray is a tool.

**Language versions.** The instructions are language-specific, so the booklet is a per-market print run. Design for a stapled booklet with a language-specific cover and body rather than printing one combined edition.

## Instruction quality is measurable

There is a simple test that almost nobody runs: give the set to an adult who has never seen it, with the instruction booklet and no help, and ask them to perform three tricks.

What you learn:

- Whether the steps are complete.
- Whether the diagrams are legible at print size.
- Whether any prop is described in a way that does not match what shipped.

The third one is the expensive one. A prop substitution between sample and production — common when a factory runs out of a component — turns a working trick into a broken one, and the instructions will not warn anyone.

## Video changes the category

Magic is the one toy category where a short video is nearly mandatory. A still photograph cannot show a vanish; it can only show a prop.

If you sell on a marketplace or your own site, budget for one clip that demonstrates the four spine props. A factory that can supply a demo clip of the actual set is worth preferring over one that supplies only stills, because the clip also proves the props work.

## Durability: what breaks

The failure pattern on magic sets:

1. **Spring and elastic props** — the elastic perishes within a season; a spare is cheap to include.
2. **Thin plastic gimmicks** — crack at the seam where they flex.
3. **Printed cards** — edges wear and the gimmick becomes visible.
4. **Metal coins and shells** — plating wears, revealing a different colour underneath, which can expose the method.

Ask for spares on the consumable props. It costs little and it converts a one-season set into a two-season one.

## Age grading

Most magic sets sit at 6+ or 8+, driven by small parts and by the motor skills needed for the sleights. Do not push a set down to 3+ for a wider market: the props are small, the tricks are not followable, and the age claim is the thing a market-surveillance officer checks first.

## What to ask before ordering

1. Full prop list, with which props are self-working and which require practice.
2. Number of printed pages in the instructions, and how many languages.
3. Whether any prop is substituted at production, and how that is communicated.
4. Spares for elastic and spring props.
5. A demo clip of the spine props.
6. Age grade and the small-parts basis for it.

A magic set is sold twice: once in the shop, on the promise, and once at home, on whether the promise holds. The second sale is the one that decides whether you reorder.
