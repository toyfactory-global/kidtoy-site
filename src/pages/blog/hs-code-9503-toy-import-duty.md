**Short answer:** almost all children's toys fall under HS heading **9503**. But the duty rate is not set at heading level — it depends on the full national tariff line (8 digits in the EU, 10 in the US), the country of origin, and any additional trade-remedy duties that apply on top of the base rate.

If you are quoting a landed cost to your buyer, that distinction is where most estimates go wrong.

## What heading 9503 actually covers

Chapter 95 of the Harmonized System covers "toys, games and sports requisites". Heading 9503 is the toy heading:

> Tricycles, scooters, pedal cars and similar wheeled toys; dolls' carriages; dolls; other toys; reduced-size models and similar recreational models; puzzles of all kinds.

In practice that includes electric bubble machines, water blasters, building blocks, plush toys, dolls, puzzles and most of what a toy distributor buys.

Two things sit *outside* 9503 even though buyers assume otherwise:

| Product | Usually classified under | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Video game consoles | 9504 | Separate heading for video games |
| Children's ride-on cars with a motor | 9503 (pedal/wheeled toys) — but electric ride-ons can be argued into 8703 | Depends on whether it is a toy or a vehicle |
| Party balloons, plain | 9505 | Festive articles |
| Backpacks sold with a toy inside | Depends on essential character | The bag may drive classification, not the toy |

That last row is the one that causes real money problems. If you ship a makeup kit in a reusable aluminium case, the case is a component of the toy and 9503 normally applies. If you ship a toy *plus* a separate retail backpack, customs may split the shipment.

## Why the 6-digit code is not enough

Everyone quotes "9503" in a quote sheet. Customs works at the national level.

- **United States** — HTSUS 9503.00.00 plus a statistical suffix (for example 9503.00.00.70). The suffix determines the rate line.
- **European Union** — TARIC 9503 00 xx xx. The last two digits matter for duty calculation and for anti-dumping surveillance.
- **UK** — the UK Global Tariff mirrors the EU structure but has its own rates.
- **Others** — most countries publish a national tariff schedule derived from HS 2022.

**The base rate is only part of the bill.** Three layers stack:

1. **MFN duty** — the standard rate for that tariff line.
2. **Preferential rate** — reduced or zero if you hold a valid certificate of origin (for example a Form A / FTA certificate). China-origin goods do not qualify for most Western preferential schemes.
3. **Trade-remedy duties** — additional duties applied to specific origins. The US Section 301 tariffs on Chinese goods are the most common example, and they apply *on top of* the MFN rate.

Because layer 3 changes by notice rather than by legislation, **any rate you read in an article (including this one) is a starting point, not a quotation.** Confirm the current rate with your customs broker or the official tariff database at the time of shipment.

## The calculation, step by step

```
Customs value (CIF)
  = goods value + international freight + insurance

Duty      = customs value x duty rate (MFN + any trade-remedy rate)
VAT/GST   = (customs value + duty) x local VAT rate
Landed    = customs value + duty + VAT + clearance fees + inland delivery
```

Two practical notes:

- **Freight is part of the customs value** in most jurisdictions (CIF basis). A $0.30/pc saving on freight also reduces duty and VAT slightly.
- **VAT is usually recoverable** if you are VAT-registered. Duty is not. When you compare suppliers, compare duty-inclusive cost, not VAT-inclusive.

## Documents that must match the code

Customs does not check your classification in isolation — it checks it against your paperwork. These must all agree:

- Commercial invoice (description, HS code, unit price, Incoterm)
- Packing list (cartons, quantities, gross/net weight, CBM)
- Bill of lading or air waybill
- Certificate of origin (if claiming a preferential rate)
- Test reports / declaration of conformity (for CE-marked markets)

A mismatch between the invoice description and the code is the single most common cause of a shipment being held. "Plastic toy" on the invoice and 9503 on the entry is fine; "household article" on the invoice and 9503 on the entry is not.

## Where factories get this wrong

We ship bubble machines, water blasters and building block sets every month, and the classification questions we get are almost always one of these three:

**1. Mixing several products under one code.** A container with bubble guns, makeup kits and plush toys does not have one HS code. Each line needs its own code on the invoice, even if the duty rate happens to be identical.

**2. Forgetting the battery.** An electric toy with a lithium cell may need additional dangerous-goods documentation for air freight. That is a transport rule, not a tariff rule, but it changes your landed cost because air freight rates for lithium products are higher.

**3. Quoting duty from a marketplace listing.** Supplier listings on B2B platforms often show a generic code. Ask for the code the factory actually uses on its export declarations — it is the one customs will have seen before.

## What to ask a supplier before you commit

Send these four questions with your inquiry. A factory that answers all four quickly is a factory that exports regularly:

1. What HS code do you declare for this item, at full national digit length?
2. What is the carton count and total CBM for my order quantity?
3. Do you provide a certificate of origin, and which one?
4. Which test reports exist for this exact item, and can you send the PDF?
