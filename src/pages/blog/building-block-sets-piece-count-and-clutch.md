**Short answer:** a 900-piece set is not automatically dearer than a 500-piece set. What you pay for is the total weight of plastic, the number of distinct moulds that had to be cut, how much of the model is electronics, and how many printed or painted parts the factory has to handle. Piece count is the number printed on the box because it sells; it is a poor guide to cost.

## Why piece count misleads

Two sets, both 500 pieces:

| | Set A | Set B |
|---|---|---|
| Average piece weight | about 1.8 g | about 0.9 g |
| Plastic per set | roughly 900 g | roughly 450 g |
| Unique moulds | 35 | 70 |
| Printed or painted parts | 3 | 14 |

Set B has twice the tooling and four times the decorating, but half the resin. Which costs more depends entirely on order quantity — at 500 units Set B is expensive because the moulds are spread over a short run; at 20,000 units the resin in Set A dominates.

That is the whole argument. Ask for grams per set and mould count before you compare two quotes.

## The four real cost drivers

**1. Grams of ABS.** Plastic is bought by the kilo. A set that looks similar in a photo can weigh 40% more once you add structural beams, tyres and a battery housing. Ask for the net weight of the finished model, not the carton weight.

**2. Mould count.** Every unique shape is a tool. A robot with rotating joints needs more unique parts than a flat mosaic, and the tooling is amortised over your order. This is why a factory's stock designs are cheaper than your own: the mould is already paid for.

**3. Electronics.** A power box, a receiver, motors and a charging board can multiply the unit cost of a set several times over, and they bring battery certification with them. A 700-piece manual set and a 500-piece motorised set are not the same product commercially.

**4. Decoration.** Pad printing and paint passes are labour. Stickers are cheaper per unit but cost you goodwill when they peel; printed parts cost more and last.

## Clutch force is the hidden quality metric

The single most useful number a block factory can give you is clutch force: the pull needed to separate two bricks.

There is no universal legal threshold — it is a factory QC metric, measured with a force gauge on a sample of bricks. What matters is consistency:

- **Too loose** — the model sheds panels when a child picks it up. This is the number one return reason on block sets.
- **Too tight** — a six-year-old cannot separate the pieces, and neither can a parent at bedtime.

Ask the factory to state a clutch force range and to hold it across reorders. Mould cavities wear; a set that was right on the first run can be loose by the fifth. If your supplier cannot name a figure, they are not measuring it.

## Tolerances and the second order

A mould that has shot 100,000 cycles is not the same mould as the one that made your sample. Wear shows up as looser clutch and slight sink marks. This is normal and not a defect — but it is why a reorder can feel different from the first delivery, and why serious buyers keep a sealed master sample to compare against.

Budget for a dimensional check on the second and third order, not just the first.

## What to ask before you compare quotes

1. Net weight of plastic per set, in grams.
2. Number of unique moulds, and whether they are stock or cut for you.
3. Clutch force range and how it is measured.
4. Number of printed or painted parts.
5. Whether electronics are included, and what battery certification they carry.
6. Carton quantity and CBM — blocks are dense, so a carton hits 20 kg before it runs out of space.

That last point matters for freight more than people expect. A heavy, compact carton costs less per unit to ship than a light, bulky one, so a dense block set can undercut a plush item on landed cost even at a higher FOB.

## The buying decision

If you are building a range, mix stock-mould sets with one or two custom elements rather than commissioning a fully new model. You get differentiation where a customer can see it — the packaging, one signature piece, the app — without paying for twenty new tools.

Two sets in the same series should share a mould family. Ours run from a 495-piece five-model kit through to an 832-piece dinosaur that rebuilds as a robot; the piece counts differ, the QC discipline does not.
