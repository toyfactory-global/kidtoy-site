**Short answer:** toy testing is quoted per item, per standard, per laboratory, so a "typical cost" is not a real number. What you can control is the sequence: test the item you will actually ship, against the market you will actually sell into, once — and not again because the artwork changed.

## Why there is no price list

A laboratory prices a test by the hours it takes and the standards it must apply. That varies enormously:

- **The standard.** EN 71-1 mechanical testing on a simple moulded toy is a different job from a battery toy that also needs EN 62115 electrical assessment.
- **The number of components.** A playset with forty parts takes longer to assess than a bubble gun with four.
- **The materials.** Painted, plated and soft materials trigger additional chemical screening.
- **The market.** Testing for the EU and the US are different standards, often run as separate reports.

Any laboratory will quote if you send the item, its component list, the age grade and the destination markets. That quote is the only number worth planning against.

## The sequence that avoids paying twice

1. **Freeze the design.** Testing a prototype that changes afterwards is money spent on a product that no longer exists.
2. **Freeze the materials.** A pigment or a resin substitution invalidates chemical results.
3. **Freeze the artwork.** The label is part of the submission in most markets; a label change can force a re-test.
4. **Decide the markets** before you test. Testing for two markets separately doubles the spend; a laboratory can often reduce overlap if it knows both up front.
5. **Test the production item**, not a hand-made sample, where the market allows it.

## The timeline, honestly

Expect roughly: sample dispatch and receipt (a week by courier), laboratory intake and queue, the test itself, then the report. The queue is the variable — a laboratory in peak season takes longer than the same test in a quiet month.

The practical advice: do not plan a testing step into the last three weeks before a ship date. If the report is late, the container does not wait, and the choice becomes shipping untested or paying for air freight later.

## What the report actually is

A report is a record that a submitted sample met a named standard on a named date, tested by a named laboratory with a named scope. It is not a certificate that the product is safe, and it is not a guarantee that the next production run is identical.

That is why buyers should read three things on the report before accepting it: the item it names, the standard version, and the laboratory's accreditation for that specific test.

## The mistake that costs the most

Testing a sample that the factory made by hand. A hand-assembled sample can pass a mechanical test that a production unit fails, because the moulding, the fit and the assembly force are different. Test what the line produces, or accept that the report describes a prototype.

The second costly mistake is testing once, then changing the packaging artwork three months later without checking whether the change affects the submission. Often it does not — but "often" is not a standard.
