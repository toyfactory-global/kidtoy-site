**Short answer:** unless the contract says otherwise, the factory owns the mould it paid for and can run it for anyone. If a tool is specific to your product, the ownership and the exclusivity have to be written down before the tool is cut — afterwards you have no leverage.

## Two very different situations

**ODM — you buy from an existing mould.** The mould already exists, several customers use it, and you are branding the output. There is nothing to own, and no exclusivity to expect. This is fast and cheap, and it is the right starting point for most first ranges.

**OEM — a tool is made for you.** Someone pays for a new mould, and that payment is the basis for an ownership claim. This is a development project: design, tooling, trial, approval, then production.

The confusion happens when a buyer assumes an ODM item is exclusive because the factory did not mention other customers.

## What a mould commitment actually means

A new tool is a fixed cost, paid once, and it only makes sense across a volume. That is why tooling conversations come with quantity commitments — the factory amortises the tool into the unit price, and needs enough units to recover it.

Two commercial shapes:

- **Tooling charged separately**, owned by the buyer, unit price lower.
- **Tooling absorbed into the unit price**, effectively owned by the factory, unit price higher.

The second is common on small developments and is not unfair — but it should be stated, because it determines what happens if you leave.

## Writing exclusivity so it means something

"Exclusive" without terms is not a clause. A usable version names:

- **What is exclusive** — the tool, the product design, or the packaging.
- **To whom** — no sales to your market, or no sales at all.
- **For how long**, and what happens on renewal.
- **What happens to existing stock** at the end of the term.
- **What happens if the volume commitment is missed** — most factories will not hold exclusivity for a product that sells 200 pieces a year.

Exclusivity is bought with volume or with money. If you want neither, expect a general product.

## The exit question

If you move suppliers, what happens to the tool? Three possibilities:

1. **The tool is yours and can be moved.** Best, and worth paying for explicitly. Moving a mould is a real operation — it has to be removed, crated, shipped and re-qualified at the new toolmaker, so "we will send the mould" is not the same as "you will be in production in a month".
2. **The tool is the factory's, and you cannot take it.** Common when the tooling was absorbed. You leave with nothing but the product knowledge.
3. **The tool is shared or ambiguous.** The worst outcome, and the most common when nobody wrote anything down.

## A practical approach for a first OEM project

- Ask whether the item uses an existing mould or needs a new one, in writing.
- If a new mould is needed, ask for the tooling cost and the amortisation quantity separately.
- Put the ownership sentence in the same document as the price, not in a later email.
- Accept that exclusivity on a small volume is unlikely, and decide whether that matters for your market.

Most first-time buyers do not need exclusivity. They need a product that works, a carton plan and a supplier who answers. Tooling ownership becomes important at the point where the product is a meaningful part of your revenue — and by then it is usually too late to negotiate it, which is why it is worth one paragraph now.
