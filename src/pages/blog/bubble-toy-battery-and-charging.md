**Short answer:** runtime is set by the motor load and the cell, not by the label. A cheap machine that runs twenty minutes on fresh cells and then fades is the most common complaint in the category, and it is decided by the battery configuration you approve at the sample stage.

## Three configurations, three ownership experiences

**AA cells, replaceable.** Cheapest to build, field-serviceable, and the cells are usually not included. The complaints are predictable: it stops mid-party, and the parent did not buy spares.

**Built-in rechargeable with USB.** Better experience, higher unit cost, and the charging port becomes a water-ingress point on an outdoor toy. A rubber cover helps and is often the first thing lost.

**Built-in non-replaceable.** Cheapest to assemble and worst to own. When the cell dies the product is finished, which is a strange outcome for a toy sold to be used outdoors.

## What "runtime" should mean

Ask for two numbers, not one:

- **Continuous runtime** on a full charge, measured until output drops noticeably — not until it stops.
- **Runtime after a storage month**, because a cell that self-discharges in the box arrives disappointing.

A machine that produces good output for twenty minutes and weak output for another forty is not a sixty-minute machine. That distinction is where most specification arguments come from.

## The battery is a motor problem

Bubble output falls as voltage drops, so the perceived end of life is when the bubbles get small, not when the motor stops. A larger tank does not help if the motor has already faded.

Symptoms worth testing on a sample:

- Output at minute one versus minute twenty.
- Whether the machine recovers after a rest (which suggests thermal rather than battery exhaustion).
- Whether the switch feels the same when the unit is warm.

## Charging ports on a wet toy

A USB port on a product used with liquid is a design compromise. What to look for:

- A cover that closes positively, not one that relies on friction.
- The port positioned away from the reservoir and above the likely waterline.
- A charge indicator that is visible, so the customer knows it is charging.

If the port is on the underside, water pools in it. That is a five-second observation at the sample stage and a return reason later.

## What to put in the listing

- Whether batteries are included.
- The cell type and how many.
- The runtime you can defend, measured the same way the customer will experience it.
- Whether the product charges by USB and what cable is included.

The single most common complaint across the whole category is "batteries not included" discovered after purchase. Saying it plainly in the first line of the description prevents most of it.

## The sourcing decision

For a gift item, the USB rechargeable version usually justifies its cost because the unboxing experience is part of the product. For a party-favour item sold at a low price, replaceable AA cells are honest and cheap — as long as the listing says so.

What to avoid is the middle: a built-in non-replaceable cell at a gift price. It combines the cost of a rechargeable with the lifetime of a disposable.
