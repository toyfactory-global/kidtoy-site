**Short answer:** a wholesale buyer is not choosing a product, they are checking a specification. Six images cover what they check: the product clean, the product in use, the packaging, the scale, the contents, and the carton.

## The six shots

1. **Clean hero on white.** The whole product, no props, correct colour. This is the image that goes into a listing and into a buyer's internal document.
2. **In use.** A child or an adult hand using it at the correct scale. Purpose: to show what it does, and how big it is.
3. **Close-up of the feature that sells it.** The bubble output, the case latch, the brush, the nozzle. One feature, in focus.
4. **The retail packaging.** The box as it will arrive, including the front panel, so the buyer can judge the shelf presence.
5. **Contents laid out.** Everything in the box, flat-lay: items, accessories, instructions. This answers "what do I actually get" in one image.
6. **The carton and its marks.** Un glamorous and disproportionately useful — it shows the export packing, the pieces per carton and the marking.

Most factories supply 1, 2, 4 and 5. Shot 6 is the one that saves a week of emails, and shot 3 is the one that improves conversion.

## Why the lifestyle shot alone loses orders

A hero lifestyle image answers "is this attractive". A buyer needs "is this specified". Without the packaging shot they cannot judge the shelf; without the flat-lay they cannot judge the value; without the carton they cannot cost the freight.

A catalogue of beautiful photographs and no packaging shots reads as a trading company. A catalogue of slightly plain photographs plus a carton shot reads as a factory — and for a wholesale buyer, that is the better signal.

## Lighting consistency

If six products in a category are photographed under six different lighting setups, the collection page looks assembled rather than designed. Two rules fix most of it:

- **Same background, same light** for every hero shot in a category.
- **Same crop ratio**, so cards align without manual work.

This is a photography decision with a layout consequence: mixed aspect ratios are why product grids end up with uneven card heights.

## Colour accuracy

The most common complaint about supplier photos is that the colour is not the product. A pink case photographed under warm light reads as salmon, and the buyer receives something they did not choose.

Shoot with a grey card, or at minimum check the colour against the physical item before delivery. In this category the colour *is* a selling point.

## What to brief

- The six shots, named, so nothing is forgotten.
- The background and crop ratio.
- Which feature is the close-up.
- That the packaging shot must be the final artwork, not a prototype.
- That no image may be retouched in a way that changes the product.

That last line matters for a practical reason: a heavily retouched photo creates a returns problem, and in B2B it creates a dispute about what was ordered.

## Using the images

Product images are also listing images, marketplace images and social assets. That argues for shooting slightly wider than the crop you need, so the same file works in a square card, a 4:5 social post and a wide banner without a re-shoot.
