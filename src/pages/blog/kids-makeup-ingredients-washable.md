**Short answer:** kids cosmetics are almost always water-soluble, because the purchase decision is made by a parent who
wants it off the sofa and off the face. "Washable" means the film breaks down with water or mild soap — it does not mean
hypoallergenic, and it does not mean every pigment rinses out of every fabric.

## Who the formula is really for

The child uses the product; the parent buys it. That single fact explains the whole category. A long-wear formula that
survives a bath is a feature on an adult product and a defect on a children's one.

So the formulator's brief is the opposite of the adult market: low adhesion, high water sensitivity, gentle surfactant
system, pigments chosen for rinseability rather than intensity.

## What "washable" promises, precisely

Usually three things:

1. **Water-soluble film.** The product emulsifies with water, so a wet cloth removes most of it.
2. **No hard-set polymers.** The resins that make adult makeup last are what make children's makeup difficult to remove.
3. **Mild cleansing.** Soap and water is enough; no remover needed.

What it does **not** promise: that a pigment will not stain a pale cotton T-shirt, or that the product is suitable for
every skin type. Those are separate claims with separate evidence.

## The claims that need evidence

| Claim | What it needs |
|---|---|
| Washable | A demonstrable formulation property; usually uncontroversial |
| Hypoallergenic | A defined testing protocol and a result, per product |
| Dermatologically tested | A named test, on a named population, with a report |
| Non-toxic | A regulatory claim in most markets; needs the file, not the adjective |
| Food-grade | Should not appear on a cosmetic at all |
| Natural / organic | Certification with a standard behind it, not a marketing choice |

A supplier who prints the right-hand column without the left has created a liability. This is the single most common
labelling problem in the category.

## Why "non-toxic" is the phrase to retire

It sounds like the safest word on the box and it is the hardest to defend. Every cosmetic ingredient is toxic at some
dose; the question is exposure. A regulator reading "non-toxic" on a children's cosmetic will ask which standard defines it
and which test demonstrates it, and there is usually no answer.

The defensible alternative is specific: name the formulation properties, name the tests that were run, and let the buyer's
compliance team conclude.

## What a buyer can check without a laboratory

- **Does it come off with water?** Apply a swatch to skin, let it dry, wipe with a damp tissue. If it smears rather than
  lifts, the film is not water-soluble.
- **Does it stain?** Apply to a white cotton scrap, leave for an hour, wash at 30 °C. Whatever remains is what your
  customer will photograph and post.
- **Does it smell of solvent?** A strong solvent smell on a children's product is a question worth asking.
- **Does the case keep it clean?** A compact that does not close properly collects fluff, and fluff in an eyeshadow pan is
  a review waiting to happen.

## Formulation versus packaging failure

Most returns in this category are packaging, not formulation. A pan that cracks in transit, a hinge that snaps, a mirror
that comes unglued, a case that will not latch. Those are engineering problems with cheap fixes — a thicker pan wall, a
metal hinge pin, a locating rib — and they are worth raising at the sample stage, because they are far cheaper to fix
before the mould is committed.

## The commercial read

Water-soluble is not a compromise; it is the category's core feature and a legitimate selling point with parents. Say it
plainly on the box: comes off with water. That sentence does more for conversion than any adjective, and it is true.
