**Short answer:** every market requires an age grade, a warning where applicable, and the identity of the party
responsible for the product in that market. The US adds a tracking label on the product and the packaging; the EU adds the
CE mark and an EU-established responsible party; Canada adds bilingual text. Missing labelling is the most common reason a
compliant product still gets held.

## Why labelling fails more often than the product

A toy that passes EN 71 in a laboratory can still be refused at the border because the box does not say who imported it.
Testing is about the object; labelling is about traceability — can an authority identify who put this on the market and
which batch it came from. Those are separate obligations and factories are much better at the first than the second.

The other reason is that labelling is artwork, and artwork is where copy gets edited late by someone who is not thinking
about regulation.

## The universal four

Almost every market wants these, in some form:

1. **Age grading** — not a marketing decision. It follows the small-parts assessment and the intended use.
2. **Warning text** where required — choking hazard for under-3 products, and market-specific warnings such as button
   battery notices.
3. **The responsible party** — manufacturer, importer or authorised representative, with an address.
4. **Batch or lot identification** — so a recall can be scoped.

If a box has none of these, it is not ready to ship anywhere, regardless of the test reports behind it.

## Market by market

**European Union.** CE mark, the name and address of the EU-established manufacturer or importer, the age warning, and
the toy identification. The EU declaration of conformity is a document rather than a label, but the importer must be able
to produce it. Warnings must be in the language(s) of the member state of sale, which in practice means a multi-language
panel is normal.

**United States.** A tracking label on the product and its packaging for children's products — this is a statutory
requirement, not a retailer preference — plus the CPSC-required warning statements, the age grade, and the country of
origin marking. The Children's Product Certificate is a separate document held by the importer.

**United Kingdom.** UKCA marking (CE continues to be accepted in Great Britain under the current arrangement), a UK
responsible person, and the same age and hazard warnings.

**Canada.** Bilingual English and French labelling, dealer name and address, and the age grading. The bilingual
requirement catches a lot of otherwise-ready packaging.

**Australia.** Age warning, importer details, and the mandatory button-battery warning where a coin cell is accessible.

**Saudi Arabia and the Gulf.** Arabic and English, plus the SABER registration references — a PCoC per model and an SCoC
per shipment.

**Brazil.** Portuguese labelling and the INMETRO registration details for regulated toy categories.

**Japan.** Japanese labelling, importer name, target age, and the ST mark where the product carries it.

## Kids cosmetics are a different label

A kids makeup set labelled as a toy is a problem in most markets. A cosmetic label needs the ingredient list in the
required nomenclature, the nominal content, a batch code, and the responsible person for that market — and in the EU that
person must be established in the EU. The warning panel and age grade still apply, but the ingredient declaration is the
part that has no equivalent on a toy.

## What to send a factory

The fastest way to get this right is to send the factory a labelling brief rather than let them use their stock artwork:

- Market and language(s).
- Age grade and the basis for it.
- The responsible party's legal name and address, exactly as it must appear.
- Any market-specific warning text, quoted verbatim.
- Barcode type, number and position.
- Whether a tracking label is required on the product itself, not only the box.

## The check that takes two minutes

Before the shipment, read the artwork PDF and ask: could a customs officer identify who is responsible for this product
in this country, what age it is for, and which batch it came from? If any of the three is missing, the labelling is not
finished — and fixing it after the container sails means a sticker applied in your warehouse or a held shipment.

## Where this sits in the order

Labelling is approved at the sample stage, not at shipping. If the artwork is wrong when the sample is approved, the wrong
artwork is approved, and production will faithfully reproduce it. The [sample checklist](/blog/toy-sample-approval-checklist/)
puts the label review in the right place — before production, when a change is still free.
