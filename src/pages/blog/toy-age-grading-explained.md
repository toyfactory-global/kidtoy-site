**Short answer:** the age grade follows the small-parts assessment and the intended use, not the appeal of the product.
If a toy has a part that fits inside the small-parts cylinder, it cannot be graded for under-3 children, however soft or
cute it is.

## The test that sets the grade

The small-parts cylinder is a truncated cylinder of a defined diameter and depth. Any component that fits entirely inside
it — before and after a drop test — is a potential choking hazard, and a toy containing one is not suitable for children
under three.

That is why the grade is a legal and safety conclusion rather than a merchandising choice. A supplier who says "we can
grade it 0+ if you want" is offering to make a false statement.

## What else feeds the grade

- **Intended use.** A craft kit with small bottles is not for a toddler even if each bottle is large, because the activity
  is not a toddler activity.
- **Electrical parts.** Battery compartments, small cells and coin cells carry their own warnings and grades.
- **Materials.** A soft TPE block can be graded for toddlers where a hard ABS block of the same size could not, because
  the hazard profile differs.
- **Market rules.** Some markets add specific requirements for certain age bands, and the label has to state the grade.

## Why so many toys are 3+

Because 3+ is the point at which most small parts stop being an absolute prohibition. A toy with a removable wheel, a
small figure, or a component that separates under a drop test is automatically 3+, regardless of how it is marketed.

This is why the "3+" on a box often looks conservative to a parent and is precisely correct to a compliance officer. The
product may well be interesting to a two-year-old; the question is whether it is safe for one.

## The mistake that costs money

Two failure modes, both common.

**Under-grading.** Marking a product 0+ or 1+ because it looks baby-friendly, without the small-parts test. This is a
labelling failure that a retailer or a customs officer can find, and it is the kind of finding that triggers a wider
review of the supplier.

**Over-grading for convenience.** Marking a product 6+ to avoid the small-parts question when it is really suitable for
younger children. That loses sales and misrepresents the product.

The honest grade is the one the test supports, and it should be documented.

## What a buyer should ask

1. What is the age grade, and what is it based on — the small-parts assessment, the intended use, or both?
2. Was the small-parts test performed on a dropped sample as well as a new one?
3. Which components separate under the drop test?
4. Does the label carry the required warning text for the grade?

A supplier who answers all four has a considered product. A supplier who answers "3+" and nothing else has a label.

## Age grading and marketing copy

The grade constrains what you can say. If a product is 3+, your listing cannot imply it is suitable for a one-year-old,
and a photo of a toddler using it creates exactly that implication. That is a marketplace compliance risk as well as a
safety one, and it is the reason product photography matters to compliance teams.

The safe rule: the people in your images should be at least as old as the grade, and the copy should not describe
abilities below it.

## The commercial upside of getting it right

A correctly graded product with a documented basis is easier to place with institutional buyers, pharmacies and
distributors, because they ask exactly this question. The documentation is not overhead — it is what makes a product
listable in the channels that pay reliably.
