**Short answer:** private label on a makeup kit means printing your artwork on the box and the case — the formulation does not change. The MOQ that applies is the **printed packaging MOQ**, which is usually higher than the stock-pack MOQ, and the label must carry fields that a toy label never needed.

## What you are actually buying

Three levels exist in this category, and suppliers blur them:

| Level | What changes | MOQ impact |
|---|---|---|
| **Stock item** | Nothing — you buy the factory's box | Standard MOQ |
| **Private label** | Your logo and artwork on box and case | Higher — print run minimum |
| **Custom formulation** | Colours, textures, scent, shade range | Much higher, plus regulatory work |

Most first orders should be level two. Level three is a brand-building exercise, not a market test, and it carries its own safety-report requirements for the new formulation.

## The MOQ logic

Printed packaging has a setup cost: plates, colour proofing, and a minimum sheet run at the printer. That is why a supplier will quote "stock box MOQ 72, custom box MOQ 500" on the same item.

Ask the question directly: **"What is the MOQ for a custom printed box, and what is the setup cost?"** A factory that only gives you one number is quoting stock packaging.

## The label fields that get missed

For a cosmetic sold to a child, these four are where first orders fail at the border or at retail onboarding:

**1. Responsible Person (EU).** A name and address established in the EU. This is not the factory and not you, unless you have an EU entity. It goes on the label. If your artwork has no space for it, the artwork is wrong.

**2. Batch code.** Every unit needs one, and it must correspond to a real production record. This is also the field that most cheap kits omit, which tells you there is no traceability.

**3. Ingredient list in the correct order.** INCI names, descending by concentration. "May contain" colourants are listed separately. Do not accept a translated ingredient list — INCI is not translated.

**4. Nominal content and shelf life.** How many grams or millilitres, and the period after opening.

If your artwork does not have room for four lines of small print, redesign the panel before you approve the print run. Changing artwork after printing is the most expensive mistake in this process.

## Claims: what to leave off

The temptation on a private label box is to write more than the stock packaging does. Resist it.

- "Non-toxic" — an unsubstantiated claim in most markets
- "Food-grade" — meaningless for a cosmetic
- "Hypoallergenic" — requires supporting testing
- "Dermatologically tested" — requires the test to exist, by name, with a report

Every adjective on the box is a claim you may be asked to evidence. The safest private label is specific rather than promotional: contents, count, colours, age guidance.

## Artwork checklist before you send files

1. Panel dimensions and bleed, in the factory's template (do not design on a guess)
2. Barcode and, if relevant, retail compliance marks — supplied by your buyer, not invented
3. All four label fields above, in the destination market's language
4. Colour proof approval before the run — get a physical or digital proof, sign it off in writing
5. Confirmation of which elements are printed on the box versus the case

## Timeline reality

A stock item ships on the factory's schedule. A private label order adds artwork rounds, proofing and a print run. Plan the sequence as: **artwork → proof approval → print → assembly → inspection**, and assume each handoff can add days, not hours. If you have a retail delivery window, work backwards from it and confirm the print lead time in writing before you commit to the window.
