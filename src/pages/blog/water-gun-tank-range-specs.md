**Short answer:** range is the headline and tank capacity is the product. A blaster that shoots 10 metres and needs
refilling every thirty seconds is a worse toy than one that shoots 7 metres and runs for ten minutes.

## Range: what the number means

A quoted range is normally the maximum horizontal distance with the nozzle at a slight upward angle, in still air, with a
full tank and a fresh battery. In a garden with a breeze the usable range is shorter, and the *useful* range — where the
stream still has enough mass to be felt rather than misted — is shorter again.

Practical translation: a 10 m claim behaves like 7–8 m in real use. That is not dishonest marketing, it is a best-case
measurement, and every manufacturer quotes it the same way. Compare like with like rather than treating the number as a
precision instrument.

## Tank capacity is the real spec

Divide the tank volume by the flow rate and you get the run time. A 300 ml tank at a high flow rate is thirty seconds of
fun and two minutes of refilling, which is what a child remembers.

Two useful derived numbers to calculate from the specification:

- **Shots per fill** — tank volume ÷ volume per shot, if the supplier quotes it.
- **Continuous run time** — tank volume ÷ flow rate.

Ask for the flow rate. A supplier who cannot give it has not characterised the product.

## Battery configuration

Electric blasters come three ways, and the choice defines ownership:

- **AA cells, replaceable.** Cheapest and field-serviceable, but the cells are usually not included, which is the single
  most common complaint on a first use. Say so clearly in the listing.
- **Built-in rechargeable, USB.** Better experience, and the charging port is a water-ingress risk. A rubber cover helps
  and is often the first thing to be lost.
- **Battery pack with a proprietary connector.** Best performance, worst replacement story.

## Pressure and nozzle type

A single nozzle produces a coherent stream with the longest range. A multi-nozzle or "burst" head produces more water in
the air and less distance. Both are legitimate; they are different products. If the listing says "high pressure", ask
whether that means a smaller nozzle aperture (faster, thinner stream) or a stronger pump (more volume). The two feel very
different in use.

## The specs that are mostly marketing

- **"1200 ml capacity"** with no flow rate — capacity without consumption is not a run time.
- **"Range 15 m"** at a 45° angle in still air, with no mention of angle or conditions.
- **"LED lights"** — relevant for evening play and for how the product photographs; irrelevant to performance.
- **"Cooling mist"** or similar comfort claims, which are usually a fine spray from a secondary nozzle.

## What to test on a sample

1. Fill it, shoot at a wall 5 m away, and see whether the stream arrives as water or as mist.
2. Time the run from full to empty at a normal firing rate.
3. Refill it and check whether the cap seals without cross-threading.
4. Drop it on grass from waist height — the tank fitting is the part that fails.
5. Leave it charged and unused for a week, then check whether the battery has self-discharged.

## Matching the spec to the buyer

A gift buyer wants range and a premium feel. A party supplies distributor wants a low unit cost and a tank that does not
leak in a carton. An event operator wants durability and a refill that a staff member can do in ten seconds. The same
factory item can serve two of those, rarely all three — which is why the specification conversation should start with the
use case rather than the number.
