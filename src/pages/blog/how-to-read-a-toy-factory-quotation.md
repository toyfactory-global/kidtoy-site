**Short answer:** a toy quotation is only usable if it carries seven things — item number, unit price, MOQ, pieces per
carton, carton dimensions, total CBM, and a production window. A quote that says "US$2.40, MOQ 500" is not a quotation,
it is a price, and you cannot cost a shipment from it.

## Why so many quotes stop at the price

A price is the easiest number to send and the only one that cannot be verified. Everything else in the quotation depends
on production records: the carton the goods actually ship in, the number of pieces in it, the CBM your forwarder bills,
and how long the line needs.

Factories that quote only a price are usually either trading companies quoting someone else's production, or factories
that have not yet checked the packing for that item. Both are reasons to ask again rather than assume.

## The seven lines

| Line | Why it matters | What goes wrong without it |
|---|---|---|
| Item number | Ties the quote to a specific product and a specific packing | You receive a similar item at a similar price and only notice at inspection |
| Unit price + trade term | FOB, CIF or EXW? The number means nothing without it | You compare an EXW price with a CIF price and think you found a bargain |
| MOQ | Sets your cash exposure per design | You plan a range of six and discover you can afford two |
| Pieces per carton | Converts quantity into cartons | You cannot price freight or warehouse space |
| Carton dimensions | Sets the CBM and the pallet fit | The shipment plan fails at the 3PL |
| Total CBM for your quantity | The number your forwarder quotes against | Freight surprises at booking |
| Production window | The only honest lead time | A seasonal order that cannot land in time |

If a supplier can give you the first four but not the last three, ask specifically: "what are the carton dimensions, the
CBM for 1,000 pieces, and the production window from deposit?" That is a normal question and a serious factory will
answer it in the same email.

## Trade terms: the line that changes the comparison

A quotation without a trade term is not comparable to anything.

- **EXW** — the goods are yours at the factory door. You arrange everything, including the truck in China.
- **FOB [port]** — the supplier delivers to the port and clears export. You pay ocean freight and destination costs.
- **CIF [port]** — the supplier pays freight and insurance to your port. You still pay duty, clearance and inland.
- **DDP [address]** — the supplier delivers duty paid to your door. Convenient, and usually the most expensive per unit
  because you cannot see what is bundled into the price.

Most toy quotations are FOB. When you compare two suppliers, convert both to the same term first — an EXW quote looks
8–12% cheaper than FOB for exactly the reason that you are now paying for the inland leg.

## What a quotation should not contain

Two things worth pushing back on.

**A duty rate.** A factory can tell you the HS heading it ships under; it cannot tell you your national tariff line or
your duty rate. If a quote says "duty 6.5%", someone is guessing on your behalf.

**A certification claim for your market.** "CE certified" on a quotation usually means the product has a test report for
some version of EN 71, not that it is compliant for your specific market and labelling. Ask for the item number, the
standard and the date.

## The reply that saves a week

Send this back to any quote you are comparing:

> Thanks. Before we proceed can you confirm: (1) carton dimensions and pieces per carton, (2) total CBM for [quantity],
> (3) production window from deposit, (4) the exact HS heading you ship under, and (5) which payment terms apply.

A supplier who answers all five in one reply is organised. A supplier who answers three and ignores two is telling you
what the rest of the order will feel like.

## Worked example

A buyer compared two quotes for a bubble machine. Supplier A: $3.10 FOB, 96 pcs per carton, carton 60 × 45 × 40 cm.
Supplier B: $3.42 FOB, 96 pcs per carton, carton 53 × 45 × 59.5 cm. On unit price A looks 9% cheaper.

The CBM tells a different story. A's carton is 0.108 m³, B's is 0.142 m³ — so B is actually the larger carton and A is
better on volume. Now reverse the numbers: if A had quoted $3.42 and B $3.10, B's unit price would be lower while its
freight per piece was 31% higher, and the landed cost would flip. That is why the carton line is not optional.
