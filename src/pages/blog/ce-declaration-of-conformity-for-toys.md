**Short answer:** a CE mark on an ordinary toy is a self-declaration. The manufacturer — or an authorised representative established in the EU — signs a declaration of conformity, and the importer is responsible for checking it. There is no central body that issues a "CE certificate" for a plastic bubble gun, and any supplier who sends you one as evidence has told you something about their compliance process.

## What the declaration is

A one- or two-page document, signed, that says: this product meets the legislation listed here, assessed against these standards, and here is who is responsible.

It is not a test report, not a certificate, and not a quality mark. It is the manufacturer's legal statement, and it is what an authority reads first.

## What has to be in it

The content requirements are set out in the toy rules and follow a standard pattern:

| Field | What it needs to say |
|---|---|
| Product identification | Item number, model, and enough description to match the shipment |
| Manufacturer | Name and address of the manufacturer, or the authorised representative in the EU |
| Legislation | The directive or regulation the declaration is made under |
| Standards | The harmonised standards applied, with edition and year |
| Signatory | Name, position, and signature of someone authorised to bind the company |
| Place and date | Where and when it was signed |

The standard edition matters. "EN 71-1" without a year is not a complete reference, because the requirements differ between editions.

## Who is allowed to sign

Someone in the manufacturer's organisation with the authority to make the statement — typically a technical or quality director, not a salesperson, and not the trading company that arranged the shipment.

If you buy through a trading company, the declaration should come from the actual manufacturer. A declaration issued by a trader who does not make the product is worth very little, because the trader cannot vouch for the production process.

## The importer's duty

Placing a toy on the EU market makes the importer responsible for:

1. Checking the declaration and that the standards listed actually cover the product.
2. Checking the CE mark is applied where required and is legible.
3. Putting the importer's own name and address on the toy or its packaging.
4. Keeping a copy of the declaration with the technical file.

That is not a formality. If the declaration is deficient, the importer carries the exposure, not the factory. This is the practical reason buyers ask for documents before the deposit rather than after the shipment.

## The errors that make one invalid

- **A product description that does not match the shipment.** "Plastic toy" for a battery-operated item with a charger is not an adequate identification.
- **Standards that do not apply.** Listing a flammability standard for a metal-bodied item, or a standard edition from a decade ago, suggests the document was copied.
- **No edition or year on the standard.**
- **Signature from someone who cannot bind the company** — or an unsigned scan with a typed name.
- **One declaration covering a whole catalogue.** A declaration covers a product, or a clearly defined family. A blanket document covering forty items with different batteries and materials is not credible.
- **Electronic signature with no control.** A PDF with a pasted image of a signature is common and is not in itself a problem, but it should be traceable to a person who has the authority.

## Does any toy need a notified body?

Some categories do — where the manufacturer cannot rely on harmonised standards alone, or where a specific rule requires third-party involvement. For the great majority of conventional toys, the route is self-declaration supported by testing at an accredited laboratory.

If a supplier claims a "notified body CE certificate" for a simple plastic toy, ask which body, which module, and see the certificate. If none appears, the claim was marketing.

## What to ask a factory for

1. The declaration of conformity, signed, with the standard editions and years.
2. The test report it references, from a laboratory you can identify.
3. Confirmation that the item number on the declaration is the item number you are buying.
4. Assurance that any material or component change will trigger a new assessment.
5. The name of the person who signs, and their role.

## The reason to care

Customs and market surveillance do check. A container held on a documentation query costs demurrage, a missed season and a customer relationship. The declaration itself takes an afternoon to produce properly, and the only reason it is ever wrong is that it was copied from a previous product without being updated.

Ask for it early. A factory that produces a clean, product-specific declaration on request is telling you something useful about how it works.
