**Short answer:** a lithium battery must pass UN 38.3 transport testing before an airline will carry it, and the shipper
needs the test summary. It is a transport document, not a toy safety certificate — a toy can be fully compliant and still
be refused for air freight.

## What UN 38.3 is

It is the UN Manual of Tests and Criteria section 38.3 — a set of abuse tests for lithium cells and batteries: altitude
simulation, thermal cycling, vibration, shock, external short circuit, impact/crush, overcharge and forced discharge.
Passing them means the battery can be offered for transport without being treated as an undeclared dangerous good.

It applies to the cell or the battery pack, not to the toy. That matters: a factory that assembles a toy around a
purchased cell has to rely on the cell maker's report, and if the cell maker never tested it, there is nothing to send an
airline.

## Sea freight versus air freight

By sea, a lithium battery shipment is regulated but manageable, and most toy shipments travel this way without drama. By
air, the requirements are stricter and the carrier will ask for documentation before booking, not after.

That asymmetry is what catches buyers in a hurry. A product that ships by sea every year cannot necessarily be flown in
December because a season was missed. The report has to exist already.

## Installed, contained, or loose

Three configurations behave differently:

- **Installed in the equipment** (the common toy case): the battery is inside the product and the product is packaged for
  retail. Documentation requirements are the lightest, but they still require the test summary.
- **Packed with the equipment**: the battery is in the box but not installed. Usually treated more strictly than installed.
- **Loose cells shipped in bulk**: the heaviest requirements, and generally not how a consumer toy is shipped.

If a supplier offers to "just take the batteries out and ship them separately", understand what that changes: it may make
the toy easier to fly and the cells harder, and it changes what arrives at your warehouse.

## What a factory should be able to tell you

Ask directly, and expect a specific answer:

1. Which cell or pack is in this item — model and capacity?
2. Is there a UN 38.3 test summary for that exact cell, and what is the report date?
3. Is the battery installed, packed with the product, or shipped separately?
4. What watt-hour rating does the pack have?

An answer of "yes it's fine, we ship these all the time" is not an answer. The report is a document with a model number
on it, and the model number has to match the cell in the product.

## Why this affects your cost, not just your logistics

Battery toys generally cost more to ship, are slower to re-stock by air, and carry a documentation burden that grows with
the number of SKUs. A catalogue with fifteen battery items needs fifteen matching files, not one generic certificate.

That is a sourcing decision as much as a compliance one. If a range is going to be replenished urgently, favour the items
whose batteries are already documented over the ones that would need air freight and a fresh report.

## The paperwork reality

An air booking typically needs the test summary plus a shipper's declaration depending on the configuration and the
carrier. Your forwarder will tell you the exact set for your lane — that is their job, and it varies by airline and
country. What the factory supplies is the battery documentation and the product information; what the shipper supplies is
the declaration.

## A planning rule

If a product is battery-operated and the season is tight, assume air freight is not available until you have seen the
UN 38.3 summary. Not because the rule is unreasonable, but because discovering it three weeks before a deadline converts
a freight problem into a missed season.
