**Short answer:** the bubble category is not one product. It is three sub-categories — machines, guns and wands — that sell through the same seasonal window at very different price points. Buying a single SKU is how buyers end up with a range that has no entry price and no gift price. And in this category more than most, the thing that generates returns is not the moulding — it is the liquid and five small mechanical details.

## What actually drives the price

**1. Number of bubble holes.** A 6-hole head produces noticeably more bubbles than a single ring, and it is usually the first spec buyers compare. More holes means a bigger motor and a bigger pump.

**2. Whether it makes music and light.** Music plus LED is the difference between a toy and a "wow" product on video. It also adds a speaker, an LED board and a small battery load — all of which show up in the price.

**3. Battery type.** This one has a shipping consequence, not just a cost one:

| Battery | Cost | Shipping impact |
|---|---|---|
| 3× AA (not included) | Lowest | None — no battery in the box |
| Built-in lithium, USB rechargeable | Higher | Lithium documentation may apply for air freight |

A USB-rechargeable machine is a better product and a harder shipment. If your channel is online and your buyers expect rechargeable, budget for the air-freight difference. For the paperwork side, see [UN38.3 for battery toys](/blog/battery-toys-un383-air-freight/).

**4. Whether solution is included.** A machine that ships with 100ml of bubble liquid is a complete gift; one that does not is a machine plus a separate purchase. Including liquid raises unit cost and carton weight — and it raises a leakage question during transit that you should ask about explicitly.

**5. Carton density.** Bubble machines are bulky. A character-shaped machine can cost more per unit in freight than its ex-factory price suggests. Always compare CBM per unit, not just unit price.

## The solution decides your reviews

This is the part of the category that buyers underestimate, and it is cheap to get right.

A bubble solution is water, a surfactant, a humectant and a small amount of polymer for film elasticity. The surfactant lets a film form, the humectant slows evaporation so the bubble survives, and the polymer helps the film stretch instead of tearing. Cheap solution cuts the polymer and the humectant: it still bubbles, for about two seconds. That is long enough to pass a check at the factory and nowhere near long enough to keep a child interested for an afternoon.

Two ways to buy it:

| | Ready-mix | Concentrate |
|---|---|---|
| What you ship | Water | Concentrate, diluted locally |
| Freight per litre of usable solution | High | Low |
| Mixing step | None | Yours, or the retailer's |
| Quality control | Fixed by the factory | Depends on the dilution |
| Best for | Small orders, retail packs | Distributors, large volumes |

Ready-mix is the practical choice for a retail pack a customer opens and uses. Concentrate suits a distributor supplying refills, because you are not paying to ship water across an ocean — but you take on the consistency question, and inconsistent mixing produces inconsistent bubbles.

The commercial point: refill solution is a repeat purchase with a better margin than the toy, because there is no moulding cost and the customer already owns the machine. A distributor who supplies both has a relationship. One who supplies only the machine has a transaction. But the refill only sells if the first bottle was good — a customer whose opening bottle produced weak bubbles does not come back for a second.

**Check it on a sample:** blow bubbles indoors with no draught and see whether they float across a room before popping; let one land on a dark surface and time it; run a machine for five minutes and look at the reservoir — foaming, or dropping as it should; leave a bottle overnight and look for separation or sediment.

Solution is also a liquid, so it carries a leak risk inside a carton and may face an air-freight restriction depending on formulation and carrier. A leaking bottle damages the artwork of every unit near it, which is why it is usually sealed separately inside the box.

## The five failure modes that bring it back

Almost every electric bubble toy failure traces to one of these. All five are visible after an afternoon of abuse and none is visible in a photograph.

**1. Solution reaching the motor.** The reservoir is separated from the motor by a seal that can leak the moment a unit is tipped. *Test:* fill it, tip it 45° in every direction, leave it tipped for ten minutes, then check the battery bay — moisture there means the seal is not a seal. *Fix at design stage:* a raised lip around the motor housing, and a reservoir that only opens upward.

**2. The switch.** Cheap tactile switches corrode in humid outdoor conditions, and a corroded switch fails intermittently — worse than failing outright, because it passes a factory check and dies in a garden. *Test:* run it on a damp morning, then leave it in a humid room overnight and try again.

**3. The reservoir fitting.** A push-fit cap is the cheapest solution and the first part to fail; when it pops off, the product stops and the floor gets wet. *Test:* fill, close, and drop it from waist height onto a hard floor, cap downward, three times. What survives is threaded or positively latched.

**4. Battery contacts.** Spring contacts lose tension when a unit is shaken, and a bubble toy is shaken constantly. *Test:* fit cells, shake for thirty seconds, then refit them three times and check whether the contact still grips.

**5. The wand and the ring.** Residue dries between uses; a rough edge or a sharp mould line accumulates it and stops picking up film. *Test:* run it, let it dry overnight unrinsed, run it again. A noticeable drop on day two is geometry or surface finish.

Two machines at the same FOB price are not the same product. Ask for **two units** rather than one: run one normally, abuse the other, and compare. That tells you more about production consistency than any inspection report.

## Seasonality — plan backwards

Bubble toys are outdoor summer products in the northern hemisphere. Retail orders land in Q1 and Q2 for a Q2–Q3 season, and production slots fill in the months before that.

Practical consequence: **if you are ordering in Q3 for a Q3 sell-in, you are late.** The useful planning question is not "when do I want stock" but "when does my buyer's planogram lock", and work back through production plus sea freight from there. Counter-seasonal markets exist, but do not build a first order on the assumption that they will absorb a seasonal mistake.

## How to build a range rather than buy a SKU

A range that works at retail usually has four roles:

| Role | Price band | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Entry / impulse | $1.50–2.00 | Drives trial, sits by the till |
| Core volume | $2.50–3.50 | The main seller, character or novelty format |
| Gift | $4–6 | Boxed, looks like a present |
| Premium | $12+ | Anchor product, makes the rest look affordable |

Order one SKU per role, not four SKUs in the same role. The premium item does not need to be your best seller — its job is to set the price reference.

## What to ask a bubble toy supplier

1. How many bubble holes, and what is the output rate?
2. Which battery type, and is it included?
3. Is bubble solution included, and what volume — ready-mix or concentrate?
4. Carton quantity, carton size and CBM
5. How the reservoir cap seals, and how the switch is rated for humid use
6. What the motor warranty position is if a unit arrives dead

That last question separates assemblers from manufacturers. A factory that has an answer has dealt with returns before.
