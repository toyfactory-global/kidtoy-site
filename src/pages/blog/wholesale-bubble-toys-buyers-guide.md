**Short answer:** the bubble category is not one product. It is three sub-categories — machines, guns and wands — that sell through the same seasonal window at very different price points. Buying a single SKU is how buyers end up with a range that has no entry price and no gift price.

## The three sub-categories

Using real ex-factory prices from our own catalogue at stated MOQ:

| Sub-category | Example | Ex-factory | MOQ | Buyer profile |
|---|---|---|---|---|

The range from $1.82 to $16.13 is roughly **9×**. A range built only from the middle misses both ends of the shelf.

## What actually drives the price

**1. Number of bubble holes.** A 6-hole head produces noticeably more bubbles than a single ring, and it is usually the first spec buyers compare. More holes means a bigger motor and a bigger pump.

**2. Whether it makes music and light.** Music plus LED is the difference between a toy and a "wow" product on video. It also adds a speaker, an LED board and a small battery load — all of which show up in the price.

**3. Battery type.** This one has a shipping consequence, not just a cost one:

| Battery | Cost | Shipping impact |
|---|---|---|
| 3× AA (not included) | Lowest | None — no battery in the box |
| Built-in lithium, USB rechargeable | Higher | Lithium documentation may apply for air freight |

A USB-rechargeable machine is a better product and a harder shipment. If your channel is online and your buyers expect rechargeable, budget for the air-freight difference.

**4. Whether solution is included.** A machine that ships with 100ml of bubble liquid is a complete gift; one that does not is a machine plus a separate purchase. Including liquid raises unit cost and carton weight — and it raises a leakage question during transit that you should ask about explicitly.

**5. Carton density.** Bubble machines are bulky. A character-shaped machine can cost more per unit in freight than its ex-factory price suggests. Always compare CBM per unit, not just unit price.

## Seasonality — plan backwards

Bubble toys are outdoor summer products in the northern hemisphere. That means retail orders land in Q1 and Q2 for a Q2–Q3 season, and production slots fill in the months before that.

Practical consequence: **if you are ordering in Q3 for a Q3 sell-in, you are late.** The useful planning question is not "when do I want stock" but "when does my buyer's planogram lock", and work back through production plus sea freight from there.

Counter-seasonal markets (southern hemisphere, gifting occasions) do exist, but do not build a first order on the assumption that they will absorb a seasonal mistake.

## How to build a range rather than buy a SKU

A range that works at retail usually has four roles:

| Role | Price band | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Entry / impulse | $1.50–2.00 | Drives trial, sits by the till |
| Core volume | $2.50–3.50 | The main seller, character or novelty format |
| Gift | $4–6 | Boxed, looks like a present |
| Premium | $12+ | Anchor product, makes the rest look affordable |

Order one SKU per role, not four SKUs in the same role. The premium item does not need to be your best seller — its job is to set the price reference.

## What to ask a bubble toy supplier

1. How many bubble holes, and what is the output rate?
2. Which battery type, and is it included?
3. Is bubble solution included, and what volume?
4. Carton quantity, carton size and CBM
5. What is the motor warranty position if a unit arrives dead?

That last question separates assemblers from manufacturers. A factory that has an answer has dealt with returns before.
