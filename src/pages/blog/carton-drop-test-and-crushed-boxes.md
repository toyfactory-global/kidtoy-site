**Short answer:** the carton is the cheapest part of the shipment and the most expensive thing to get wrong. Three specifications decide whether it survives: board grade, stacking strength and moisture resistance. Almost no buyer asks for any of them.

## Reading the damage pattern

Where the carton failed tells you why:

| Pattern | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Corners crushed and rounded | Impact in handling; too little edge protection |
| Sides bulging outward | Stacking load from above, or contents too loose |
| Top and bottom collapsed | Stack height exceeded board strength |
| Soft, wavy, delaminated board | Moisture — container sweat or a humid warehouse |
| Puncture or tear | Handling with forks; board too thin for the weight |
| Retail box crushed, carton intact | Not enough void fill inside the carton |

That last line is the most common and the least understood: the outer carton can pass while the product inside is written off, because the colour box had nothing to stop it moving.

## Board grades, in plain terms

Corrugated board is described by the number of layers and by its strength.

- **Single wall** — three layers: liner, fluting, liner. The default for light toys.
- **Double wall** — five layers. For heavy or fragile loads, or for stacking in a warehouse.
- **Triple wall** — rare in toys; used for industrial loads.

Strength is stated two ways:

**ECT (edge crush test)** — the load a strip of board takes on its edge, in pounds per inch. This is the number that predicts stacking performance, which is the one that matters in a container.

**Burst strength (Mullen)** — resistance to puncture. Useful as a secondary figure.

If a factory quotes "good quality carton" with no grade, ask for the ECT value and the flute type. A carton that handled a 15 kg toy well is not necessarily right for a 22 kg one.

## The drop test

A drop test simulates the worst moment: the carton coming off a pallet or a tailgate.

The common protocols differ in ambition:

- **A basic integrity drop** — drops from a height set by the carton's weight, on a corner, an edge and a face. Proves the pack survives a fall.
- **A distribution simulation** — adds vibration, compression and repeated drops, approximating a real parcel journey.

For a container shipment, the compression and stacking part matters more than the drop. For a parcel or e-commerce shipment, vibration and repeated drops matter more, because the carton is handled many times.

Ask which protocol was run and at what weight and drop height. "Drop tested" alone is not a specification.

## The stacking calculation nobody does

Stacking strength falls as humidity rises. A carton that holds four high in an air-conditioned warehouse may collapse three high in a humid container after three weeks at sea.

Three practical rules:

1. **Specify for the worst case.** Design the stack height for the humidity on the route, not for your warehouse.
2. **Reduce the stack.** One fewer carton per stack is cheaper than a claim.
3. **Use edge protectors and a level pallet.** Load spread across the corners is what the board is designed for; load concentrated in the middle of a face is not.

## Void fill is not optional

Inside the carton, the retail box must not move. A millimetre of travel becomes an impact after a thousand kilometres of vibration.

Cheap, effective options: a folded cardboard divider, a moulded pulp end cap, or a snug fit achieved by right-sizing the carton. The last one is free — it only requires ordering the correct carton size rather than the nearest stock size.

Bubble wrap and air pillows work but add material weight, which matters if you are also declaring packaging under an EPR scheme.

## Moisture: the invisible failure

Containers sweat. Warm humid air inside, cold steel outside, condensation on the walls and on the top layer of cartons.

Mitigations that actually work:

- **Desiccant** in the container, sized to the volume and the voyage.
- **Liner or kraft paper** between the carton and the container wall.
- **Avoid loading against the doors and walls** where condensation collects.
- **Ventilated or moisture-resistant board** for long routes.

A carton that arrives soft but not broken is a warning: the contents have been in a humid environment for weeks, and printed packaging inside may already be warped.

## What to specify in the purchase order

1. Board construction: single or double wall, flute type.
2. ECT value, in writing.
3. Carton dimensions, internal, and the tolerance.
4. Drop test protocol and the drop height, or acceptance of the factory's standard.
5. Void fill type and whether it is included in the unit price.
6. Printing and marking requirements, including any handling symbols.
7. Desiccant and liner responsibility, if the route is long.

## The cost argument

Upgrading board from one grade to the next usually adds a small amount per carton — often less than the value of a single damaged unit inside it. The comparison to make is not carton price against carton price. It is carton price against the cost of the units that arrive unsaleable, plus the customer conversation, plus the replacement freight.

That comparison almost always favours the better board, and it is the one specification most buyers never see.
