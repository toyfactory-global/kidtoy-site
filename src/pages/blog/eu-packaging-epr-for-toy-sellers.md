**Short answer:** if you place packaged goods on an EU market, you are a producer of packaging for the purposes of extended producer responsibility. That means registration in the country, reporting the packaging you put on the market by material and weight, and paying a fee. Marketplaces now ask for the registration number before they let a listing go live.

## Why this hits toy sellers specifically

Toys are the worst-case packaging category:

- **Heavy on packaging.** Colour box, insert tray, poly bag, tie-downs, sometimes a display carton around the retail box.
- **Mixed materials.** Card, printed paper, plastic film, sometimes foam. Each material is a separate fee line, and mixed laminates are the most expensive to recycle and the most expensive to declare.
- **Sold into multiple countries.** Each country has its own registration, its own reporting and its own fee schedule.

A toy that sells for a few dollars can carry packaging that is a meaningful share of the landed cost before the product cost is even counted.

## What the obligation actually is

The structure is consistent across member states even where the details differ:

1. **Register** with the national scheme in each country where you place packaged goods on the market — either directly or through an authorised representative.
2. **Declare** the packaging you place on the market, by material type and weight.
3. **Pay** a fee based on that declaration, and on the recyclability of the material.
4. **Label**, where the country requires sorting information on the pack.

Who is the "producer" depends on the country and the sales model. If you sell directly to consumers in a country, it is generally you. If you sell to a distributor who puts their own brand on it, it may be them. This is the first question to settle, because getting it wrong means registering in the wrong place.

## The marketplace check

The practical reason this moved from a compliance footnote to an operational blocker: large marketplaces now require a registration number for the markets where packaging EPR applies, before a listing can be created.

If you have ever had a listing refused for a missing compliance number, this was probably it. The number has to be valid, in the right country, for the right material categories.

## How pack design decides the fee

The fee follows weight and material, so pack design is a cost decision, not only a branding one.

| Design choice | Effect on EPR |
|---|---|
| Single-material card box | Simplest to declare, usually lowest fee band |
| Card box plus plastic window | Two materials, two lines, and a plastic fee |
| Mixed laminate film | Higher fee; harder to recycle |
| Foam insert | Additional material, usually a poor fee band |
| Shrink wrap over a box | Small weight, but a separate plastic line |
| Printed leaflets in multiple languages | Paper, but weight accumulates across a run |

The cheapest pack from a printing quote is not always the cheapest after EPR, and the difference is large enough to matter on a low-price item.

## Design changes that pay for themselves

- **Drop the window.** Replace a plastic window with a printed image. Saves a material line and a plastic fee.
- **Replace foam with moulded pulp or folded card.** Same protection in most cases, better fee band.
- **One language per market.** Multi-language leaflets add paper weight and, more importantly, are wasted on markets that do not read them.
- **Right-size the box.** Over-sized boxes are the most common avoidable cost: more board, more void fill, more fee.
- **Avoid mixed laminates** unless the barrier is genuinely needed. For most toys it is not.

## What to do before you list

1. Identify every country you will sell into, and the sales model for each.
2. Confirm who the producer is for each country — you, or your buyer.
3. Register where required, and record the number where your listings need it.
4. Build a packaging bill of materials by material and weight, per SKU. You need this to declare, and you need it to forecast the fee.
5. Add the label requirements for the countries that require sorting information on pack.

## What to ask a factory

1. The packaging bill of materials, by material and weight, per SKU.
2. Whether plastic can be removed or reduced without losing protection — this usually requires a drop test to prove.
3. Whether the box can be right-sized for the retail unit rather than the carton.
4. Artwork files with a separate layer for country-specific sorting marks.

Rules, fee schedules and registration thresholds change, and they differ by country. Confirm the current position for each market you sell into rather than relying on a summary — including this one.
