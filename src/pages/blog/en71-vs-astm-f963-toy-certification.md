**Short answer:** the standard you need depends on where you sell, not on what the factory prefers. Selling into the EU requires CE marking supported by EN71 testing. Selling into the US requires compliance with ASTM F963 under CPSIA, plus a Children's Product Certificate. Selling into both means you need both — one does not substitute for the other.

## The two routes, side by side

| | European Union | United States |
|---|---|---|
| Legal framework | Toy Safety Directive 2009/48/EC | CPSIA (Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act) |
| Technical standard | EN71 series | ASTM F963 |
| How compliance is shown | CE mark + EU Declaration of Conformity + technical file | Children's Product Certificate (CPC) |
| Who tests | Self-declaration allowed, but testing is expected in practice | Third-party lab accepted by the CPSC — mandatory |
| Who holds the file | Importer / authorised representative in the EU | Importer or domestic manufacturer |

The structural difference matters: **in the EU, the importer carries the legal responsibility for the CE mark.** A Chinese factory can test and provide reports, but it cannot issue a CE declaration on your behalf for goods you place on the EU market. In the US, the CPC is likewise issued by the importer or the domestic manufacturer — not by the overseas factory.

If a supplier tells you "we will give you the CE certificate", the accurate version of that sentence is "we will give you EN71 test reports and a declaration of conformity you can use as the basis for your own."

## What EN71 actually consists of

EN71 is not one test. It is a series, and different parts apply to different toys:

| Part | Covers | Applies to |
|---|---|---|
| EN71-1 | Mechanical and physical properties — small parts, sharp edges, drop and tension tests | All toys |
| EN71-2 | Flammability | All toys; strictest for soft/plush and costume items |
| EN71-3 | Migration of certain elements (heavy metals) from toy materials | All toys, tested by material type |
| EN71-4 / -5 | Chemical experiment sets and chemical toys | Only those product types |
| EN71-7 | Finger paints | Only finger paints |
| EN71-8 | Activity toys for domestic use (swings, slides) | Only that category |
| EN71-14 | Trampolines | Only trampolines |

For a plastic bubble machine or a water blaster, you are looking at **EN71-1, -2 and -3** and nothing else from the series. If a spec sheet claims "EN71" without naming the parts, ask which parts were tested.

## What ASTM F963 covers

ASTM F963 is the US toy safety specification. It covers mechanical and physical properties, flammability, heavy metals in substrate materials, and — importantly — it is **mandatory** under CPSIA, not optional. A toy sold in the US without F963 compliance is an illegally sold toy, regardless of what a test report says.

Alongside it, CPSIA adds chemical limits that sit outside F963:

- **Lead:** 100 ppm in substrate materials, 90 ppm in paint and surface coatings
- **Phthalates:** 0.1% each for the eight restricted phthalates in children's toys and childcare articles
- **Third-party testing** by a CPSC-accepted laboratory, with periodic re-testing

Those lead figures were reviewed again in 2026 and left unchanged. They are limits on the finished product, not on the components — a compliant paint on a non-compliant substrate still fails.

## Decoding the abbreviations on a Chinese spec sheet

This is where most buyers lose the thread. These codes appear on almost every supplier specification sheet and almost none of them are "certificates":

| Code | What it actually means |
|---|---|
| **10P** | Ten phthalates tested. Usually the eight CPSIA-restricted phthalates plus DINP and DIDP. |
| **HR4040** | Shorthand for the US CPSIA 2008 amendment — on a Chinese spec sheet it almost always means the lead limits, not a full CPSIA certificate. |
| **AZO** | Azo dyes / aromatic amines restricted under EU REACH. Relevant to textiles, paint and printed film. |
| **EN62115** | Safety of electric toys. Applies to anything with a battery or motor — bubble machines and water blasters included. |
| **CD** | Cadmium. A separate heavy-metal limit from lead. |
| **PAHS** | Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. Relevant where rubber or soft plastic touches skin — grips, handles, soft blocks. |

So a line reading **"EN71 · 10P · ASTM · HR4040 · AZO · EN62115 · CD · PAHS"** means: EN71 parts tested, ten phthalates tested, ASTM F963 tested, US lead limits met, azo dyes tested, electric toy safety tested, cadmium tested, PAH tested. That is a genuinely thorough scope — but it is eight separate tests, not one certificate.

## How to read the report, not the claim

Ask for the PDF and check four things:

1. **The item number on the report matches the item you are buying.** A report for "bubble gun, model A" does not cover model B, even if they look identical.
2. **The lab is accredited and, for the US, CPSC-accepted.** The lab name should be recognisable — Intertek, SGS, BV, TÜV, CTI.
3. **The report date is recent enough for your buyer.** Most retailers and marketplaces ask for a report within 12 months.
4. **The tested scope matches the material.** EN71-3 is tested per material colour and type. A report covering two colours does not automatically cover a third.
