**Short answer:** if a product goes on the skin or lips, it is a cosmetic in most jurisdictions regardless of who it is
marketed to. A toy safety certificate does not cover it. The regime that applies is the cosmetic one, and it brings an
ingredient file, a responsible person and a different label.

## The mistake

A buyer orders a kids makeup kit, receives a CE declaration and an EN 71 report, and lists it. Months later a marketplace
or a pharmacy chain asks for the cosmetic notification and there isn't one. The product is fine; the paperwork answers a
different question.

This is not a technicality. Toy regulation asks: is this object safe for a child to handle and mouth? Cosmetic regulation
asks: what happens when this substance sits on skin, and who is accountable for that claim in this market? Different risk,
different regulator, different document.

## What "cosmetic" means in practice

If the product's purpose is to colour, cleanse, protect or perfume the human body — including lips, nails and eyelids —
it is treated as a cosmetic. A play makeup set with eyeshadow, lip gloss and nail polish is squarely in that category even
though the packaging says "toy" and the age grade says 3+.

Some products straddle both. A makeup *case* with no cosmetic inside is a toy. A case with a lip gloss inside is both a
toy and a cosmetic product, and needs both files. When a buyer asks us whether an item ships to a given market, that is the
question we answer first.

## The four documents a cosmetic needs

**1. A product information file.** The formulation, the safety assessment, the manufacturing method, the stability and
microbiological data. In the EU this file must be available to the authority on request, held by the responsible person.

**2. A responsible person.** In the EU, an entity established in the EU, named on the label, who holds the file and
answers to the regulator. A factory in Guangdong cannot be that party — this is the point where most first-time
programmes stall, because it needs an agency or a partner before the label can be finalised.

**3. A notification or registration.** The EU requires the product to be notified via CPNP before it is placed on the
market. The US has its own registration and listing obligations for cosmetic facilities and products under current FDA
rules. These are administrative steps with lead times, not paperwork you do at the port.

**4. A compliant label.** Ingredient list in the required nomenclature, nominal content, batch code, responsible person,
and any warnings. Not the same panel as a toy.

## Where a toy certificate still matters

The case, the mirror, the brushes, the moulded tray — anything that is not applied to the body — is a toy or a general
product and needs the corresponding safety file. A makeup set therefore often needs both a toy file for the packaging
components and a cosmetic file for the contents. Buyers who ask for "the certificate" in the singular are already in
trouble.

## The claims that get products withdrawn

Words like **non-toxic**, **food-grade**, **hypoallergenic** and **dermatologically tested** are regulated claims in most
markets. Each one implies evidence: a test, an assessment, a definition with legal weight. A factory that prints
"non-toxic" on a box because it sounds reassuring has created a liability for the importer.

The defensible path is narrower and slower: state what the product is made of, state what it has been tested for, and let
the evidence carry the claim. If the evidence does not exist, the words should not be on the box.

## What to ask a supplier, in order

1. Is this product a cosmetic in my market, and which items in the set are cosmetic versus toy?
2. What formulation documentation exists, and can it support a product information file?
3. Which laboratory did the microbiological and stability testing, and when?
4. Who will be the responsible person, and what do they need from the factory?
5. What exactly will the label say, in which language?

If a supplier answers question one with "it's a toy", that is the moment to slow down. It may be an honest answer for a
particular item, but it should be a considered one.

## The commercial consequence

Pharmacy chains and gift retailers with a compliance function will not list a kids cosmetic without the file. That is not
obstruction; it is the condition of selling in that channel. The buyers who plan for it get listed in one cycle. The buyers
who discover it after the order was placed spend the season explaining a delay.
