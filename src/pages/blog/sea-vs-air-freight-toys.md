**Short answer:** air freight is worth paying for when the goods are needed before a date that sea cannot meet, when the
value-to-weight ratio is high enough to absorb it, or when the alternative is a stock-out on a proven seller. It is
almost never worth it for a first order of a cheap, bulky toy.

## The basic comparison

| | Sea (FCL) | Sea (LCL) | Air |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transit time | 20–35 days port to port | 25–40 days | 3–7 days |
| Cost basis | Per container | Per CBM | Per kg (chargeable weight) |
| Best for | Volume, low value density | Small shipments | Urgency, high value density |
| Complexity | Moderate | Moderate, more handling | Higher documentation |

For toys, the critical detail is that air is priced on **chargeable weight**, which is the greater of actual weight and
volumetric weight. A bulky light toy is charged on its volume even though it weighs almost nothing, which is why air
freight on bubble machines is disproportionately expensive.

## The arithmetic that matters

Two numbers decide it.

**Freight per unit.** Total freight ÷ pieces. Compare the air figure with the sea figure and then with the retail price.
If air adds 30% to the landed cost, and the margin cannot absorb it, air is not an option — it is a decision to sell at a
loss.

**The cost of being late.** If the goods arrive three weeks after the season starts, what do you lose? Not just the sale —
the shelf space, the promotion, and the reorder. If the season is the whole business, air can be cheaper than late even at
five times the freight.

That second number is the one buyers skip, and it is usually larger than the first.

## When air is clearly right

- **Replenishment of a proven seller** that is about to go out of stock during peak.
- **Samples and pre-production units** that need to be in someone's hands for approval.
- **High-value-to-weight products** — small electronics, craft kits — where the freight is a small share of the goods
  value.
- **A launch with a fixed announcement date** that cannot move.

## When air is clearly wrong

- **First orders.** You are still learning the product, the packaging and the demand. Paying a premium to accelerate an
  unproven decision is expensive.
- **Bulky, low-value items.** If freight exceeds the goods value, the model does not work by air.
- **Anything with a battery that has no UN 38.3 report.** It will not fly at all.
- **Liquid-filled products** without carrier documentation. Also frequently restricted.

## The middle options buyers forget

- **Sea-air.** A sea leg to a hub then air to destination. Slower than air, cheaper than air, faster than sea. Available
  on some lanes.
- **Split shipment.** Send a small air quantity to cover the first weeks and the balance by sea. This is the standard
  answer to a tight season and it is almost always cheaper than flying everything.
- **Express courier** for genuinely small volumes, where the per-kg rate is high but the fixed costs are zero.

## Planning backwards, honestly

If the shelf date is fixed, count: production 15–25 days, plus air 3–7 days plus clearance 3–5 days, or sea 20–35 days
plus clearance 7–10. The difference is roughly three to four weeks. Decide which one you are buying before you place the
order, because the decision at booking time is not a decision — it is a rescue.

## A practical rule

Use sea for the base load and air only for the gap. Model the gap explicitly: how many units do you need in the first
three weeks, and what does it cost to fly exactly those? Buyers who plan the gap pay a small premium on a small quantity
rather than a large premium on the whole order.
