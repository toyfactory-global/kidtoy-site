**Short answer:** two makeup kits can contain almost identical cosmetics and sell at three times the price difference, because the case is what the customer judges. When you compare suppliers on "number of colours", you are comparing the wrong variable. Choose the case first, then the contents, then the range.

## The five formats, in price order

Using our own catalogue as a reference point — these are real ex-factory prices at their stated MOQ, not estimates:

| Format | Example | Ex-factory | MOQ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Printed box / treasure box | Pink vanity treasure box, 4-colour shadow | **$6.42** | 108 |
| Suitcase (soft/hard plastic) | Blue makeup suitcase, 9-colour | **$6.83** | 80 |
| Iron case with mirror | Unicorn vanity case, 12-colour, 7 brushes | **$8.75** | 60 |
| Aluminium case, laser surface | 3-layer laser case, 18-colour | **$12.69** | 100 |
| Aluminium trolley case | 2-layer glitter trolley, 14-colour | **$23.03** | 60 |

The spread is **3.6×** — from $6.42 to $23.03 — on products that all contain eyeshadow and lip gloss. Nothing about the cosmetics explains that gap.

## What actually drives the price

**1. Layers.** A single-layer case opens to one tray. A three-layer case opens like a tackle box into three working surfaces. Each layer adds tooling, assembly time and volume — and it is the single biggest driver of perceived value on a shelf.

**2. The surface treatment.** Plain ABS is cheap. Laser-etched aluminium looks expensive from two metres away. Glitter laminate sits between the two. This is where an $8 case becomes a $23 case with almost no change in contents.

**3. Whether it has a mirror and a light.** An LED mirror converts a case into a "vanity" — different product, different shelf, different price. It also adds a battery, which changes your shipping classification.

**4. Case dimensions and carton efficiency.** A trolley case is bulky. Fewer units per carton means higher freight per unit, which shows up in your landed cost even when the ex-factory price looks reasonable.

**5. MOQ behaviour.** Notice the pattern above: the cheapest format has the *highest* MOQ (108), and the most expensive has one of the lowest (60). Expensive formats are lower-volume by nature; factories set MOQ accordingly.

## What the case is made of

Before price, material — because it decides what the product *is* in the customer's mind.

| Format | Cost | Gift appeal | Durability | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Printed card box | Lowest | Low | Dents, corners crush | Entry price, multipacks |
| ABS or PP plastic case | Low–mid | Mid | Good, scratches visible | Kids' first kit, marketplace |
| Aluminium case | Mid–high | High | Very good, hinge is the weak point | Gift retail, planogram |
| Iron / tinplate vanity | High | Highest | Best, heavy | Premium gift, trolley formats |

**Why aluminium reads as premium.** A thin aluminium case has a metallic sheen, a satisfying weight in the hand, and a latch that clicks. None of that is expensive to produce, but together it changes the object's category: it stops being a toy with makeup in it and becomes a makeup case for a child. The trade-off is that aluminium dents, and a case that arrives dented in a retail carton is unsaleable. Inner packing matters more with aluminium than with any other format.

**Why card is still right for some ranges.** A printed card box at a low price point is not a bad product; it is a different product. It works for a multipack, a party favour, a marketplace listing where the box is photographed and then discarded, and any price point under roughly $6 retail where a case would eat the margin. The mistake is a card box at an aluminium price, or an aluminium case at a card price with freight that was never costed.

## The three parts that fail

1. **The hinge.** On an aluminium case it is usually a piano hinge or two pins; on an ABS case it is a living hinge. A living hinge has a finite number of cycles and will crack — that is a design choice, not a defect.
2. **The mirror.** Glued mirrors come unglued in a warm container. A mirror held by a frame costs more and survives.
3. **The latch.** A friction latch that loosens over time is the difference between a case that closes and one that spills in a bag.

Ask how each one is fixed. A supplier who can describe the hinge construction is a supplier who has thought about returns.

## Choosing a format, by channel

| Your channel | Sensible format | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Discount retail, high volume | Printed box or soft suitcase | Lowest unit cost, best carton density |
| Mid-market gift | Iron case with mirror | Reads as a gift, still under ten dollars |
| Department store / boutique | Aluminium, 2–3 layer | The case is the display object |
| Online marketplace hero SKU | Trolley case | Photographs well, high perceived value per thumbnail |
| Party favour / low ticket | Bag format | Lowest landed cost, sold in multiples |
| Pharmacy planogram | Consistent case footprint | Must stack; avoid trolley formats |

## Building a range instead of buying a kit

A successful kids beauty range is four to five SKUs at different price points, each playing a role:

| Role | Example | Ex-factory | MOQ |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Entry / impulse** | Unicorn rainbow backpack kit | $5.95 | 108 |
| **Core volume** | Treasure box vanity, 4-colour | $6.42 | 108 |
| **Gift** | Premium complete set with jewellery | $6.88 | 72 |
| **Mid-premium** | Mermaid aluminium case with LED mirror | $9.92 | 120 |
| **Premium / anchor** | 2-layer glitter trolley case | $23.03 | 60 |

Four to five SKUs across that ladder gives a buyer a reason to take a mixed order. Four SKUs all at $6.50 gives them a reason to take one.

And the shelf works by comparison: the premium item is not there to be the best seller, it is there to make the core item look like good value. Remove the anchor and the core price looks expensive. If everything in your range is a $6 kit, you have one product in four packagings — no entry price for a trial buyer, no gift option for a higher-ticket channel.

The column buyers forget is carton density: premium formats are bulky, so freight per unit is worse even when the ex-factory price is fair. Compare **landed** cost per unit across the range, not ex-factory.

## The mistake buyers make

Ordering the expensive format first. A $23 trolley case at MOQ 60 ties up more capital per SKU than a $6.42 treasure box at MOQ 108, and the expensive format is the harder sell in an unproven market.

The sequence that works: **start with the mid format, prove sell-through, then add the premium format as a second SKU in the same range.** Two SKUs at different price points also give your buyer an anchor — the $23 case makes the $12 case look reasonable.

## A shipping consequence worth flagging early

Any case with an LED mirror contains a battery. That changes air freight eligibility and cost, and it may change the dangerous-goods documentation your forwarder asks for. If you are comparing a mirror case against a non-mirror case, compare the **landed** cost, not the ex-factory price.

Cases are also hollow, so they ship inefficiently: volume without much weight. Carton dimensions and whether the inner packing nests are real cost levers — a case that nests saves container space, a case with a fixed handle does not. Raise it at the sample stage, because inner-packing design is cheap to change before production and expensive afterwards.

## What to ask before you commit

1. Case material and thickness (ABS, iron, aluminium, laminated board)
2. Whether the surface is painted, laminated or laser-etched
3. Number of layers and the working surface area when open
4. Carton quantity and CBM — the trolley format will surprise you here
5. Whether the mirror is LED-lit, and if so, the battery type
6. How the hinge, mirror and latch are each fixed

On a sample: open and close the case fifty times and look for hinge play, latch looseness and mirror movement; drop it closed from waist height; then put a weight on it and leave it — the shape should recover or not deform.
