**Short answer:** total US duty on a Chinese-made toy is the general HTSUS rate for that line, plus any additional duty imposed under Section 301, plus any other action in force. None of the three is fixed. The only reliable method is to look up your own 10-digit line in the current HTSUS and check Chapter 99 for the Chapter 99 subheadings the line cross-references.

We are not going to print a percentage here, on purpose. Rates change by Federal Register notice, and a number frozen into a blog post is worse than no number.

## Why nobody can give you "the toy tariff"

Three layers stack:

1. **The base rate** in HTSUS Chapter 95 for your specific 10-digit line. This is the duty that applies to every origin.
2. **Section 301 additional duty** on goods of Chinese origin. The lists are long, product-specific, and have been modified repeatedly; each Tranche has its own Chapter 99 subheading.
3. **Other actions in force at the time of entry.** Various measures have applied to Chinese-origin goods over the past several years, and they change.

Then there is the fourth factor that is not a duty at all: your **entry value**. Duty is charged on declared value, so how you declare — FOB versus a higher transaction value — changes the money.

## The lookup method

This is the sequence a customs broker runs, and it is not difficult to do yourself for a sanity check.

**Step 1 — classify.** Find your 10-digit HTSUS line. A battery-operated bubble machine and a set of building blocks are both under heading 9503, but not necessarily the same statistical suffix. The suffix determines the rate line.

**Step 2 — read the base rate.** Column 1 General applies to normal trade relations origins. Note whether the line is free or has a percentage.

**Step 3 — check Chapter 99.** The current HTSUS includes notes and subheadings that impose the additional duties. The chapter also carries the cross-references telling you which Chapter 99 subheading applies to which Chapter 95 line for Chinese origin. This is where the Section 301 duty lives.

**Step 4 — check for exclusions or modifications.** Exclusions have been granted and have expired by product and by date. An exclusion that applied to a shipment last year may not apply to the next one.

**Step 5 — confirm with your broker before the deposit.** A broker with your classification and origin will give you the rate for that shipment. That is the only number that binds.

## What this means for quoting

If you quote a landed cost with a duty rate you cannot re-verify, you are quoting an assumption. Three habits make it safer:

- **State the rate and the date you looked it up** in your costing sheet. When it changes, you know which quotes to rework.
- **Build a duty variance line** into the costing rather than pretending the rate is stable. See [import costs buyers forget](/blog/toy-import-costs-beyond-freight/).
- **Do not let a supplier's "duty included" DDP quote hide the rate.** Ask for the breakdown, because a DDP price with an outdated duty assumption is a price that will be revised upward.

## The classification question is often the bigger one

Section 301 gets the attention, but classification errors cost more over a year. The recurring ones on toys:

| Situation | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Toy packed in a reusable case | The case may or may not change classification |
| Toy plus a separate bag or backpack | Essential character argument; customs may split |
| Battery included, toy plus charger | The charger can have its own line |
| Party goods with a toy | Heading 9505 versus 9503 |
| Ride-on with a motor | Toy or vehicle — a large rate difference |

A wrong suffix on a consistent basis is a pattern customs can assess retroactively. A written classification decision from a broker, kept on file, is cheap insurance.

## Origin is not always obvious

Country of origin is not where the goods shipped from. Substantial transformation matters: moulding parts in one country and assembling in another can leave origin unchanged. If any step happens outside China, get the origin position in writing before assuming the Section 301 duty applies — or does not.

## A practical checklist before you quote a US buyer

1. 10-digit HTSUS line, in writing, with the reasoning.
2. Base rate for that line, dated.
3. Chapter 99 subheading applied, dated.
4. Any current exclusion, with its expiry date.
5. Origin position documented if any process is outside China.
6. Duty variance allowance in the costing.
7. Broker's confirmation per shipment, not per year.

The rate is a moving part. Treat it like freight: re-check it each time you book, and never let a number from last season set a price for this one.
