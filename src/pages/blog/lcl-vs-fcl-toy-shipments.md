**Short answer:** LCL is priced per CBM plus fixed charges per shipment, so it rewards small volumes and punishes the
fixed cost. Once the volume approaches roughly half a 20GP on many lanes, a full container is cheaper per CBM — but the
crossover moves with the lane, the season and the surcharges, so it has to be priced rather than guessed.

## Why there is no universal break-even

The rule you hear — "above 15 CBM book a 20GP" — is a rough average, not a law. The crossover depends on:

- The lane. A congested route with high terminal charges pushes the crossover down; a quiet route pushes it up.
- Season. Peak season surcharges hit FCL and LCL differently.
- Origin charges. LCL carries per-shipment fees (documentation, CFS handling, consolidation) that a full container does not.
- Destination charges. LCL deconsolidation fees at destination are frequently larger than buyers expect.

That is why the honest answer is: get both quotes for your actual CBM, on your actual lane, in your actual month.

## What LCL is good for

- A **first order** where you are testing a supplier and do not want a container's worth of exposure.
- A **replenishment** of one or two fast-moving items.
- A **sample-plus-small-stock** shipment where speed of decision matters more than freight per piece.
- Any shipment where the fixed charges are small relative to the goods value — which means higher-value, lower-volume
  goods, not cheap bulky toys.

## Where LCL hurts toys specifically

Toys are bulky and light. That is the worst combination for LCL, because LCL is priced on volume and the fixed charges are
spread over a small number of cubic metres. A 6 CBM toy shipment can carry a freight cost per piece that is two or three
times the FCL equivalent.

The other LCL problem is handling. A shared container means your cartons are loaded and unloaded alongside other
shippers' cargo, which increases the chance of crushed corners — and toys are usually in printed retail boxes where a
crushed corner is an unsaleable unit.

## The arithmetic to run

For each option, calculate:

1. **Freight per CBM** — total freight ÷ CBM.
2. **Total landed freight** — freight plus origin and destination fixed charges.
3. **Freight per piece** — total landed freight ÷ pieces.
4. **Fill percentage** if FCL — CBM ÷ practical container capacity.

The fourth number tells you what you are paying for; the third tells you what it costs. If FCL is 40% full, you are buying
60% empty space, and it may still be cheaper per piece than LCL — but you should know that is what you are doing.

## The middle option nobody quotes

A **mixed container with another buyer**, or a **co-loaded container** arranged by a consolidator, sits between the two.
Some factories can offer this when two orders on the same lane are close in time. It is worth asking, particularly for a
first shipment that is too big for comfortable LCL and too small for a sensible FCL.

## A practical decision rule

- Under about 8 CBM: LCL is usually right, and speed matters more than the freight per piece.
- 8–15 CBM: price both; this is where the answer flips and where the lane decides.
- Above about 15–20 CBM: FCL is almost always right, and the question becomes which container size.
- Above 30 CBM: you are choosing between a 40GP and a 40HQ, and the HQ is usually the better use of the freight.

Run the volume through the [container loading calculator](/tools/container-loading-calculator/) first, then ask a forwarder
for both quotes on that exact number. Guessing at the break-even is how buyers end up paying LCL rates for a load that
would have fitted a container.
