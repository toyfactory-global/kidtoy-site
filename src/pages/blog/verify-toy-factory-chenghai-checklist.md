**Short answer:** verify the legal entity, then the physical production line, then the export history — in that order. Most buyers run these backwards: they visit a showroom, see samples, and wire a deposit before checking whether the company they are paying actually owns a moulding machine.

Chenghai, in Shantou, Guangdong, is the densest toy production cluster in China. That density is useful — you can visit twenty suppliers in two days — and dangerous, because a trading company can rent an office in the same building as a factory and look identical from the outside.

Here are eight checks, ordered by how much they cost you to run.

## 1. Match the legal name to the bank account

Before anything else: the company name on the proforma invoice, the name on the business licence, and the name on the bank account must be **the same legal entity**.

This one check eliminates most deposit fraud. A supplier who asks you to pay a personal account, or a Hong Kong account belonging to a different company name, has just told you something important.

Ask for the business licence (营业执照) as a scan. The unified social credit code is verifiable, and the registered capital and establishment date are on the document.

## 2. Ask for the factory address, then check it on a map

A trading company will give you an office address — usually a floor in a commercial building. A factory gives you an industrial address, often a road or village name in a specific industrial zone.

Drop the address into a satellite map. What you are looking for:

- A building with a loading bay, not a shopfront
- Roof area consistent with a factory (extraction, water tanks)
- Not the same building that appears on twelve other suppliers' profiles

## 3. Request a live video walkthrough, not a video file

A recorded factory tour can be licensed, borrowed or years old. A live call cannot.

Ask for a video call at a specific time and request these shots in one continuous take:

1. The entrance with the company sign
2. The moulding floor with machines running
3. The assembly line with your product category being worked on
4. The QC area with the inspection bench
5. The finished-goods warehouse with cartons

If the call drops every time you ask for the warehouse, that is the answer.

## 4. Check whether the item you want is actually their mould

This is the check buyers skip most often. Two factories can quote the same bubble gun because one bought it from the other.

Ask: **"Is this your own mould? How many cavities, and what is the mould number?"** A real manufacturer answers immediately. A trader goes quiet, then says they will check.

You can also ask for a photo of the mould itself. A factory that owns tooling is not shy about showing it.

## 5. Verify the test reports against the item number

Ask for the PDF report and confirm the item number on the report matches the item you are ordering — not the category, the item.

Check the issuing lab, the test scope, and the date. A report covering EN71-1/-2/-3 for a different model tells you the factory tests, not that this product passed.

## 6. Order a paid sample before discussing volume

A free sample is a marketing expense. A paid sample at the quoted unit price plus freight is a transaction, and transactions reveal how a supplier behaves.

Order two units. You are checking:

- Does the sample match the photos and the spec sheet?
- How long did it actually take to arrive?
- Was the packaging the retail packaging you were promised, or a plain carton?
- Did they send tracking without being chased?

Suppliers who are slow and vague on a $30 sample are slow and vague on a $30,000 order.

## 7. Ask what happens when a carton fails inspection

Not "do you have QC" — everyone says yes. Ask the specific question: **"If your own inspection finds a defect rate above the agreed AQL, what happens?"**

Good answers describe a concrete process: rework on their line, replacement cartons, a re-inspection before shipment, and who pays for the delay. Bad answers describe a promise.

Our own policy is a 7-day pre-shipment inspection window, and defects are handled under a written refund policy rather than an ad-hoc discount negotiation.

## 8. Get one reference in your own market

Ask for the name of one buyer they ship to in your region, in your product category. You are not asking for a confidential customer list — you are asking whether they can produce a single verifiable reference.

A factory that has exported for years can usually name a market and a channel ("we supply a distributor in Poland for bubble toys"). A trading company that has never shipped will change the subject.

## The order matters

Run them in this sequence and you will spend about two weeks and very little money:

| Order | Check | Time | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Legal name vs bank account | 10 minutes | 0 |
| 2 | Factory address on a map | 15 minutes | 0 |
| 3 | Live video walkthrough | 30 minutes | 0 |
| 4 | Mould ownership | Same call | 0 |
| 5 | Test reports for the exact item | 1–2 days | 0 |
| 6 | Paid sample | 1–2 weeks | Sample + freight |
| 7 | Defect process | Same call as 3 | 0 |
| 8 | Regional reference | 1–2 days | 0 |

Steps 1 to 5 cost you nothing but attention, and they are the ones that catch the cases that actually hurt.
