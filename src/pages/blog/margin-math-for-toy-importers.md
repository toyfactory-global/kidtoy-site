**Short answer:** gross margin is what is left after the goods. Net margin is what is left after everything else — damage, markdowns, storage, payment fees, returns and your own time. On a low-value toy line the gap between the two is often larger than the margin itself.

## The worked example

Take 500 units landed at $4.74 each, sold wholesale at $6.77 — the landed cost example from our calculator. Gross margin per unit is $2.03, about 30%.

Now subtract what actually happens over a season:

| Item | Effect on the 500 units |
|---|---|
| Damage and unsaleable units (a few percent) | a handful of units at full cost |
| Markdown to clear the last of the stock | a discount on the tail of the run |
| Storage while the stock sells | a monthly cost per pallet |
| Payment and currency fees | a small percentage of revenue |
| Sample, inspection and freight admin | a fixed sum per order |
| Your own selling time | the largest uncosted item |

The order still makes money. But the 30% is not 30%, and the honest number is usually in the high teens — which is fine, as long as it was planned rather than discovered.

## Why toys are exposed

Three structural reasons:

**Low unit value, high handling cost.** Every unit has to be picked, packed and shipped. A $2 toy and a $40 toy cost about the same to handle, so the cheap one carries a much larger share of the overhead.

**Bulky relative to value.** Storage and freight per dollar of revenue are high, so the fixed costs bite harder than in a denser category.

**Seasonality.** Stock that misses its window has to be marked down or held for a year, and both are expensive.

## The numbers to model before the order

1. **Landed cost per unit** — not FOB.
2. **Freight and duty variance** — a buffer for a rate move or a reclassification.
3. **Damage allowance** — a percentage, based on your packaging, not on optimism.
4. **Markdown allowance** — the discount you will need to clear the tail.
5. **Storage months** — how long the stock actually sits.
6. **Channel fees** — marketplace commission, fulfilment, or a distributor's margin.

If the residual after all six is not worth the capital, the order is not a good order, however attractive the unit price looked.

## The markdown trap

A seasonal toy bought at a good price and marked down 30% to clear can lose the entire gross margin and part of the cost. That is why the order size matters more than the price: buying 20% too much usually costs more than buying 5% too dear.

Buy to the container, size to the season, and reorder rather than over-committing. Reordering costs a lead time; over-ordering costs cash.

## A simple discipline

Before placing an order, write the expected net margin down — after damage, markdown, storage and fees. Then, after the season, write the actual next to it. Two or three cycles of that give you a model of your own business that is worth more than any industry benchmark.
