**Short answer:** on a painted figure, the plastic is the cheap part. Tooling and paint passes are the cost, and paint adhesion is the quality risk. A figure with four paint passes can cost more than one twice its size with one.

## Tooling decides what is possible

A figure with realistic shapes — overlapping plates, an open jaw, curved claws — needs split moulds with slides. Every undercut is a mechanical action in the tool, and every action raises the tool cost and the cycle time.

That is why:

- **Simple cartoon shapes** are cheap: a two-part mould, no slides, a short cycle.
- **Realistic dinosaurs** are not: slides, cores, and often a separately moulded jaw or limb.
- **Very large figures** need thicker walls or hollow sections to avoid sink marks, which changes the mould again.

If a buyer sends a reference photo and asks for "this but cheaper", the honest answer is usually to simplify the silhouette, not to thin the plastic. Thinner walls fail the drop and torque tests.

## Paint is where the labour goes

Spraying is done in a booth, usually by hand with a mask or a stencil, and each colour pass is a separate operation with drying time between passes.

Count the passes on a good figure: base colour, back or stripe, belly, eyes, claws, teeth. That is five or six operations, each with handling, drying and rejection risk.

Cheaper alternatives and their trade-offs:

| Method | Cost | Weak point |
|---|---|---|
| Hand spray with stencil | Moderate | Labour; consistency between units |
| Pad printing | Low per colour | Flat-ish surfaces only |
| Tampo plus spray on details | Moderate | Registration between passes |
| Single-colour moulded plastic | Lowest | Looks like a toy, not a model |
| Decal or sticker | Lowest | Peels; fails abrasion |

For a figure sold at a low price point, moulded colour with one or two printed details is the right answer. For a collector-facing figure, pay for the passes — the paint is the product.

## Adhesion is the test that matters

Paint that flakes off is not just a cosmetic complaint. A flake of paint is a small part, and in a toy for children under three that is a safety issue, not a quality issue.

Three checks worth asking for:

1. **Cross-hatch or tape test.** A grid is cut into the paint and tape is applied and pulled. Anything that lifts is a fail.
2. **Abrasion rub.** A defined rub with a cloth or eraser, checking for transfer.
3. **Wet and dry handling.** Paint that survives dry handling can fail after a child's hands are wet.

Ask the factory what adhesion test they run and at what frequency. Factories that spray in-house usually have one. Factories that subcontract painting sometimes do not.

## Safety: two areas specific to figures

**Small parts.** Claws, horns and tails are the classic break-off shapes. The relevant tests are torque and tension on any projection, plus a drop test on the whole figure. Design out thin projections where possible; a thicker claw usually costs nothing in material and passes.

**Surface migration.** Painted surfaces are tested as supplied, including the paint layer. This is why the paint supplier's documentation matters as much as the resin's. Buying the cheapest paint on the market is a false economy on a product that will be tested.

Age grading interacts with both. A figure with breakable parts is not a 3+ item regardless of what the artwork says — see our guide on [age grading](/blog/toy-age-grading-explained/) for how that call is made.

## Size, weight and freight

Figures are dense and awkward. A carton of large dinosaurs cubes out before it hits a weight limit, which pushes freight cost up per unit. Two practical notes:

- **Hollow versus solid.** Hollow mouldings use less material but need a second tool operation and can crack at seams.
- **Carton packing.** Individual poly bags cost money and time but reduce paint rub in transit. For a painted figure, the bag is not optional.

## What to ask before ordering

1. Tool: how many parts, how many slides, and is it stock or new.
2. Number of paint passes and the method for each.
3. Adhesion test method and frequency.
4. Which paint system, and whether its documentation is on file.
5. Carton quantity, CBM and gross weight — figures are heavy for their size.
6. Whether individual bagging is included.

A dinosaur sells on its silhouette and its paint. Everything else in this article is about making sure the silhouette arrives intact and the paint stays on it.
