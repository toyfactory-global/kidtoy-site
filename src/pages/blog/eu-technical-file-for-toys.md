**Short answer:** the technical file is the evidence behind the CE mark. It is assembled by the manufacturer, kept for ten years, and has to be producible on request to a market-surveillance authority. It is not the same document as the test report, and a folder of certificates is not a technical file.

## What has to be in it

The framework is set out in the toy safety rules currently applying in the EU. The component list is stable across revisions:

| Component | What it has to show |
|---|---|
| Product description | What it is, model or item number, colourways, age grade |
| Safety assessment | An analysis of the hazards for the intended age, and how each is addressed |
| Conformity assessment | Evidence the applicable essential requirements were assessed |
| Declaration of conformity | Signed, current, matching this product |
| Test reports | From an accredited lab, against the current standard editions |
| Production control | How the factory keeps the tested article identical to what ships |

The last row is the one that separates a real file from a stack of paper. A test report proves a sample passed. Production control proves every unit is that sample.

## The four gaps that fail most often

**1. The report does not cover the shipment.**
A report for the blue colourway, a different material, or a similar model number is not evidence for the product in the box. Testing families exist and are legitimate — but the report has to list the models and materials it covers, and your shipment has to be inside that scope.

**2. The age grade in the file differs from the packaging.**
If the report is written for 3+ and the box says 2+, the file is not evidence for the product as sold. This is checked first because it takes ten seconds.

**3. The standards are out of date.**
Standards get revised. A report against a superseded edition may not be accepted, and the transition period for the new edition is a date you have to know rather than assume.

**4. Nothing shows production control.**
No internal inspection records, no material certificates, no record of which batch the tested sample came from. Without this, an officer is entitled to ask whether the tested sample was representative — and the honest answer is often "we don't know".

## Who holds it, and who can be asked for it

The manufacturer assembles it. The importer placing the toy on the EU market must be able to produce a copy on request. In practice, that means:

- The file should be as complete at the importer as it is at the factory, in a language the authority can read.
- The importer's name and address must appear on the toy or its packaging.
- The file has to be retained for ten years after the last unit is placed on the market.

If you buy through a trading company rather than a factory, confirm in the purchase contract who is responsible for producing the file. This is the single most common gap in trading-company supply chains, and the importer carries the obligation regardless of what the supplier promised.

## The framework is in transition

The EU has been moving from the existing Toy Safety Directive to a Toy Safety Regulation. Regulations apply directly, without national transposition, and the replacement changes some obligations.

Do not rely on a summary — including this one — for the transitional dates. Two things are worth doing instead:

1. **Ask your testing lab** which instrument and which transitional position applies to your product and your placing-on-market date.
2. **Ask your importer** whether their compliance process has been updated for the change.

Everything in the component table above survives the transition in some form. The dates, the documentation details and the market-surveillance fees do not.

## Building a file that survives a check

- **One file per product family**, with an index page. An officer who has to open forty PDFs will ask harder questions.
- **A material table.** Every plastic, paint, ink and textile, with its supplier and its test status.
- **The sample record.** Which production batch the tested sample came from, and where the retained sample is kept.
- **A change log.** Any material or supplier change after testing, and the re-test decision. This is what catches factories out: a resin supplier change silently invalidates the basis of the report.
- **Language.** The safety assessment and instructions in the language of the market you are selling into.

## What to request from a factory before you order

1. The technical file index, not just a certificate scan.
2. Test reports naming the models and materials they cover, with standard editions and dates.
3. The internal production-control procedure, and a recent inspection record.
4. Confirmation that any material change will be notified to you in writing before shipment.
5. The age grade, stated once, and used identically on the report, the DoC, the box and the listing.

A technical file is not a marketing asset and it never helps sell a container. It helps exactly once — when someone asks for it and you can hand it over.
