**Short answer:** an AQL inspection samples a shipment and accepts or rejects it on a defect count. It is not a guarantee of zero defects — it is an agreed definition of "acceptable", and having that definition before production is what makes a defect conversation short instead of endless.

## What AQL means without the statistics

AQL stands for acceptable quality limit. In practice: you agree defect categories (critical, major, minor), a sample size based on the batch, and a maximum number of defects allowed in the sample. If the sample exceeds it, the batch fails and is reworked or rejected.

The important consequence: a passed inspection means the batch is within the agreed limit. It does not mean every unit is perfect. On a 1,000-piece order, a handful of minor defects is a pass under most sampling plans — and that is a number to agree deliberately, not to discover later.

## Critical, major, minor

- **Critical** — unsafe, or would break a legal requirement: sharp edges, a missing warning, a battery compartment a child can open. Usually zero tolerance.
- **Major** — does not function, or would be returned by a customer: a machine that does not blow bubbles, a case that will not latch.
- **Minor** — cosmetic or packaging: a scuff, a print misalignment, a dented box corner.

Most disputes are about the boundary between major and minor. Writing an example of each into the purchase order fixes that boundary before the argument exists.

## Why the inspection beats the claim

A claim after arrival is expensive for both sides. The return freight usually exceeds the value of the defective units, so the factory cannot economically take them back, and the buyer cannot economically send them. The negotiation ends in a credit note on the next order, which is only useful if there is a next order.

An inspection before shipment is cheap by comparison and happens while the goods are still in a place where they can be fixed.

## What a factory needs to act on a claim

If a defect is found after arrival, the claim is only actionable with evidence:

- The item number and the production batch.
- Clear photographs of the defect, several units, not one.
- The count: how many units affected, out of how many received.
- The carton numbers, so the batch can be traced.
- Whether the units are available for return or were already sold.

A message saying "the quality is bad" cannot be answered. A message with a batch, a count and photographs usually can.

## The claim window

Almost every supplier has an implicit or explicit window after arrival in which a claim is considered. Confirm it before ordering. The reason is practical: a factory cannot investigate a complaint about a production run from eight months ago, because the mould, the material batch and the line records have all moved on.

Ask for the window in writing, and inspect early. A claim raised in week one is a quality conversation; the same claim in month four is a discount request.

## The commercial reality

This is B2B wholesale, and no factory offers unconditional returns — the economics do not allow it. What a serious factory offers instead is: a sample you can abuse, an inspection you can book, and a claim process that responds when the evidence is there.

If a supplier promises free returns on a container, ask who pays the freight. The answer tells you whether the promise is real.
