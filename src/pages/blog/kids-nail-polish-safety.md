**Short answer:** a kids nail polish is a cosmetic in the markets that matter, so the label follows the cosmetic rules, not the toy rules. The formulation choice — water-based peel-off versus solvent — decides the label, the shipping class and how the product behaves in a child's hands.

## The two formulations

**Water-based, peel-off.** Dries by evaporation of water, lifts off in a sheet when it is rubbed. No strong solvent smell, removes without remover, and the failure mode is that it comes off earlier than a child wants.

**Solvent-based.** Dries hard, lasts longer, and needs a remover. It brings a solvent smell, flammability considerations for transport, and a formulation story that a parent will ask about.

For a children's product the water-based version is easier to defend and easier to ship. The trade-off is durability, which is why kid polishes are sold as a fun, temporary thing rather than a long-wear product.

## Why "cosmetic" changes everything

If it goes on a nail, most jurisdictions treat it as a cosmetic. That means:

- An ingredient declaration on the label, in the required nomenclature.
- A responsible person for the market — in the EU, established in the EU.
- Product information file obligations for the formulation.
- Batch identification, so a recall can be scoped.

A toy safety report does not cover any of that. A set containing polish can need both files if the case and the applicators are also toys.

## Transport

A water-based polish is far easier to ship by air than a solvent-based one, because the solvent version may be classified as a flammable liquid depending on its flash point. That is not a detail to discover at the airport: it changes the freight mode, the packaging and sometimes the carrier.

If you are sourcing polish for a market that needs fast replenishment, the formulation is a logistics decision as much as a product one.

## Three claims to avoid

**"Non-toxic."** A regulatory claim in most markets with no common definition. It invites a question about which standard and which test.

**"Food-grade."** Should not appear on a cosmetic at all.

**"Safe for children"** without an age grade and a basis. A cosmetic intended for children still needs the age context.

What to print instead: what it is made of, that it is water-based and peels off, and the age it is intended for. Those are verifiable and they answer the parent's actual question.

## What to check on a sample

- Apply it, let it dry, and time how long it takes to lift at the edges.
- Test whether it stains a pale fabric — the claim that matters most to a parent.
- Smell it. A strong solvent smell on a children's product is a reason to ask what is in it.
- Check the brush and the bottle: a stiff brush or a bottle that will not close is a return.

## The commercial read

Kids nail polish sells as part of a set, not as a standalone, and its job in that set is to make the whole thing look like real makeup. That means the case and the arrangement matter as much as the formula — and the formula has to be defensible when a parent reads the label.

If a supplier cannot tell you whether the polish is water-based or solvent-based, they cannot tell you the shipping class either. That is the question to start with.
