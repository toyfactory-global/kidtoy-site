**Short answer:** anything that requires opening a carton and touching each unit should be done at the factory. Anything that requires an Amazon-specific label should be done close to the fulfilment centre, because the requirements change and a whole container of pre-applied labels cannot be corrected.

That line is where most FBA toy sellers either overspend or get rejected.

## The split

**Do at the factory**

- Poly bagging, with the suffocation warning printed on the bag.
- Retail box sealing, if the box does not hold closed.
- Inner packing that protects the product in transit.
- The retail barcode, printed on the packaging.
- Carton-level marks: item number, quantity, weight, dimensions.

**Do near the fulfilment centre**

- The FNSKU label, unless you are certain of the requirements and the label placement.
- Box-content labels for sets and multipacks.
- Any prep step that depends on the current policy, because that policy is updated periodically and a container takes weeks to arrive.

## The choke hazard warning

A poly bag above a certain size must carry a suffocation warning. Requirements differ by market and by marketplace, and the wording is prescribed closely enough that paraphrasing it is a mistake.

Ask the factory to print it as part of the bag artwork rather than applying a sticker afterwards. A sticker applied in a warehouse is a labour cost and a source of inconsistency.

## Sets and multipacks

A set sold as one unit must be marked as a set, and must not be able to be split in a way that makes the listing untrue. If three items are sold together, they need to arrive together, in packaging that says so, with a label identifying the set.

The commercial risk is a customer receiving part of a set and filing a claim — which is not a packaging aesthetic question, it is a listing accuracy question.

## Toys and compliance, before prep

Prep is the last step, and it cannot fix a compliance problem. If the product needs a Children's Product Certificate, a tracking label, or an age warning, those are production-time decisions. A perfectly prepped shipment of a product without the required labelling is still a shipment that cannot be sold.

This is the ordering that catches sellers new to the category: they organise the fulfilment centre, the prep and the photography, then discover the compliance gap three weeks before launch.

## What to write into the purchase order

- The exact poly bag specification and warning text, as a file.
- Whether the retail barcode is printed or applied, and where.
- The carton marks your inbound shipment needs.
- A statement that the factory will not apply marketplace-specific labels unless instructed in writing and confirmed against the current requirements.

That last line protects both sides: it stops the factory from guessing, and it stops you from inheriting a guess.

## A practical order of operations

1. Confirm the product is listable in your market (labelling, testing, age grade).
2. Confirm the packaging and the retail barcode.
3. Confirm the carton and the inbound marks.
4. Ship.
5. Apply marketplace labels at the destination, or at a prep centre, where a change is cheap.

Steps 1 to 3 are factory conversations. Steps 4 and 5 are logistics. Mixing them — asking a factory to solve a marketplace label question — is how containers end up sitting while somebody reads a policy page.
