**Short answer:** duty and freight are the costs everyone budgets. The ones that break an order are the charges that only
appear when something goes wrong — demurrage, detention, terminal handling, examination fees, and the rework cost of a
labelling mistake. Most of them are avoidable with a week of planning.

## The full stack

| Cost | When it appears | Avoidable? |
|---|---|---|
| Goods (FOB) | On the purchase order | Yes — by negotiating |
| Ocean / air freight | At booking | Partly |
| Marine insurance | At booking, small | No |
| Import duty | At clearance | No, but the tariff line matters |
| VAT / GST | At clearance | Recoverable if registered |
| Customs brokerage | Per entry | No |
| Terminal handling / THC | At destination port | No |
| Demurrage | Container sits in port past free time | Yes — plan the pickup |
| Detention | Container held past free time after pickup | Yes — return it on time |
| Examination / inspection fee | Customs selects the container | Not directly |
| Re-labelling or re-packing | Wrong or missing label | Yes — get artwork right first |
| Inland haulage | Port to warehouse | Partly |
| Warehouse handling | Per pallet in | No |

## Demurrage and detention, explained plainly

Two different clocks, and buyers routinely confuse them.

**Demurrage** is charged when the container stays inside the terminal beyond the free days — you have not collected it.
**Detention** is charged when you keep the container outside the terminal beyond the free days — you have collected it but
not returned it.

Both are charged per container per day, they escalate, and the free period is typically short at busy ports. A container
that arrives while your warehouse is full, or over a public holiday, can cost more in demurrage than the freight on the
goods. If a shipment lands before you can receive it, that is a planning failure, not a bad-luck event.

## The label mistake that costs a container

If the warning panel is wrong or the importer details are missing, the options at the port are: hold the container and
argue, re-export, or re-label locally. Re-labelling means unpacking cartons, applying stickers, repacking and reprinting
carton marks — in a customs-bonded or third-party facility, at an hourly rate, with a delay attached.

The cost of getting the artwork right before production is a few hours of someone's attention. The cost of getting it
wrong is a four-figure bill plus a missed shelf date. This is the clearest case in importing where the cheap step is the
one that matters.

## Duty: the part that depends on the tariff line

Duty is a percentage of the customs value, usually the CIF value. The percentage depends on the national tariff line, not
on the 6-digit HS heading — two products that both sit under 9503 can carry different rates in the same country.

The practical consequence: the classification is a decision, and it should be made by someone qualified, before the goods
ship. A broker who classifies on arrival is guessing at the same moment the clock starts running.

## The invisible costs of a small order

Small orders carry a disproportionate share of the fixed costs — brokerage, terminal handling, documentation, and often a
minimum freight charge. That is the structural reason a small order can cost 30–50% more per unit even at an identical FOB
price. It is not a supplier penalty; it is arithmetic.

This is the strongest argument for consolidating: two small orders on the same lane in the same month should be one
shipment, because the second shipment buys almost nothing for its fixed cost.

## What to budget, as a check

For a first sea shipment, a rough planning allowance beyond the goods and freight is: duty at your tariff rate,
insurance at a fraction of a percent, brokerage and terminal charges as a fixed sum in the low hundreds, and inland
haulage. If your landed-cost model produces a number that feels too good, one of those is missing.

The [landed cost calculator](/tools/landed-cost-calculator/) has a field for "other fixed costs" precisely for this — put
the brokerage, documentation and terminal charges in there, and the answer stops flattering you.

## The three-week checklist

- Tariff line agreed with a broker before production ends.
- Label artwork signed off at the sample stage.
- Warehouse capacity confirmed for the arrival week.
- Free-time dates diarised the moment the bill of lading is issued.
- Broker briefed with the invoice and packing list before the vessel arrives.

Five items, none of them technical, and between them they eliminate most of the costs in the table above that a buyer can
actually control.
