**Short answer:** a lip gloss sold to a six-year-old is a **cosmetic**, not a toy, even though it sits in the toy aisle and is bought as a gift. Treating it as a toy is the single most common compliance mistake in this category — and it is the one that gets shipments held.

## The classification problem

Toy safety rules exist to protect children from mechanical and chemical hazards in *play*. Cosmetic rules exist to protect the skin and eyes of the person using the product.

A children's makeup kit is both, in the sense that it is played with, but legally it is a cosmetic. In the EU that means the **Cosmetics Regulation**, not the Toy Safety Directive. In the US it means FDA cosmetic rules, plus CPSIA obligations because the user is a child.

Why this matters commercially:

| If you treat it as a toy | If you treat it as a cosmetic |
|---|---|
| You ask the factory for EN71 / ASTM reports only | You also need a cosmetic safety report and ingredient disclosure |
| You put your own brand on the box and ship | You need a Responsible Person in the destination market |
| You claim "safe, non-toxic, food-grade" freely | Every claim must be substantiated or you are in breach |
| You test one unit per colour | You need batch-level traceability |

## The claims problem — read this before you approve artwork

This is where most importers get caught, and it costs nothing to avoid.

**"Non-toxic" is a risky claim.** In the EU, cosmetic claims must be substantiated under the Common Criteria for cosmetic claims. "Non-toxic" implies a guarantee about absence of harm that you cannot evidence on a label. Regulators treat it as an unsubstantiated claim. The same applies to "hypoallergenic" and "safe for all skin types" without testing to support them.

**"Food-grade" is worse.** It is meaningless for a cosmetic and invites the question of why you would compare a cosmetic to food at all.

**"Washable" is defensible — if you can show it.** Washability is a physical property you can test (removal with water at a defined temperature, staining on fabric). If the factory claims washable, ask for the test method, not just the word.

What you can safely say, and what actually sells to a wholesale buyer:

- The ingredient list, in the required order
- Batch number and shelf life
- Age guidance you are prepared to defend
- That the product is manufactured under a documented quality system

## What the EU requires

- A **Responsible Person** established in the EU, named on the label. The Chinese factory cannot be the Responsible Person.
- A **Product Information File** per product, held by the Responsible Person.
- **CPNP notification** before placing on the market.
- A **Cosmetic Product Safety Report** signed by a qualified safety assessor.
- INCI ingredient labelling, batch code, and nominal content.

None of this is done by the factory on your behalf. The factory supplies formulation and test data; the legal obligation sits with the brand.

## What the US requires

- FDA cosmetic labelling: ingredient declaration, net quantity, identity of the distributor.
- Because the user is a child under 12, **CPSIA** applies as well — including lead limits in the product and the packaging.
- **MoCRA** introduced facility registration and product listing obligations for cosmetics. Confirm the current requirements with your regulatory consultant, as implementation timelines have been staged.

## What to ask a supplier before ordering

1. **"Is there a cosmetic safety report for this exact formulation?"** Not for the category — this formula.
2. **"What is the full ingredient list, and which colourants are used?"** Colourants are the most common compliance failure in cheap kits.
3. **"What is the batch coding and shelf life?"** If they cannot answer, there is no traceability system.
4. **"Can you supply the product without the words 'non-toxic' or 'food-grade' on the packaging?"** A factory that pushes back here does not understand the category.
