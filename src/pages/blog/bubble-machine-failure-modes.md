**Short answer:** almost every electric bubble toy failure traces back to one of five things — solution reaching the
motor, a switch that corrodes, a reservoir fitting that pops off, a battery contact that loses tension, or a wand that
clogs. All five are visible after an afternoon of abuse, and none is visible in a photograph.

## 1. Solution reaching the motor

The reservoir sits below or beside the motor, separated by a seal or a wall. A seal designed on a bench in a clean
environment can leak the moment a child tips the unit.

**Test:** fill it, tip it 45° in every direction, and leave it tipped for ten minutes. Then check the battery bay. Moisture
in the battery bay means the seal is not a seal.

**Fix at the design stage:** a raised lip around the motor housing, and a reservoir that only opens upward.

## 2. The switch

Cheap tactile switches corrode in humid outdoor conditions, and a corroded switch behaves intermittently — which is worse
than failing outright, because it passes a factory check and fails in the customer's garden.

**Test:** run it outdoors on a damp morning, or in a bathroom after a shower, and see whether the switch still feels
positive. Then leave the unit in a humid room overnight and try again.

## 3. The reservoir fitting

A push-fit cap is the cheapest solution and the first part to fail. When it pops off, the product stops working and the
customer's floor gets wet.

**Test:** fill, close, and drop the unit from waist height onto a hard floor, cap downward. Do it three times. A fitting
that survives is a threaded one or has a positive latch.

## 4. Battery contacts

Spring contacts lose tension when a unit is shaken — and a bubble toy is shaken constantly. The symptom is a product that
works, then stops, then works again when the child taps it.

**Test:** fit cells, shake the unit for thirty seconds, and see whether it cuts out. Then remove and refit the cells three
times and check whether the contact still grips.

## 5. The wand and the bubble ring

Solution residue dries on the wand between uses. A wand with a rough edge or a ring with a sharp mould line accumulates
residue and stops picking up film properly.

**Test:** run it, let it dry overnight without rinsing, then run it again. If the output drops noticeably on the second
day, the wand geometry or the surface finish is the problem.

## What this means for a purchase decision

Two machines at the same FOB price are not the same product. The difference is usually invisible in the specification and
obvious after a week of use. That is why the sample stage matters more in this category than in almost any other toy
category — and why a supplier who will not send a sample to be abused is telling you something.

Ask for two units rather than one. Run one normally and abuse the other. The comparison tells you more about the
production consistency than any inspection report.

## The commercial consequence

A 3% field failure rate sounds acceptable until you count the cost: a replacement unit, the return shipping, the support
time, and the review. On a low-value item the review is the expensive part, because it is public and it affects every
future sale of that listing.

That is the argument for buying a slightly more expensive unit with a better seal rather than the cheapest one on the
quotation. The failure rate is not a quality preference; it is a line item.
